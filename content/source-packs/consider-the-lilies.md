## 2. Lexicon (verified against live BibleHub interlinear/Strong's, 2026-08-26)

**μεριμνάω (merimnaō) — G3309, "worry / be anxious."** Present Imperative Active in Matthew 6:25,27,28,31,34 AND Philippians 4:6 (μηδὲν μεριμνᾶτε) — the _identical grammatical form_ bridges both anchor passages; this is a genuine lexical bridge, not a loose thematic one.
**Honesty note (downgrade, per the Jabez-precedent standard):** popular devotional lexicons (HELPS Word-studies) claim merimnaō derives from merizō ("to divide"), glossed "drawn in opposite directions" — the origin of the "worry is a divided mind" preaching point. Checked against Beekes' _Etymological Dictionary of Greek_ (2010, via Wiktionary's sourced etymology entry): the word traces instead to a Proto-Indo-European root _(s)mer-_ ("to remember/care"), cognate with Latin _memor_ ("mindful," root of "memory") — a "care-full remembering," not a "divided mind." **Use "divided mind" only as an illustrative metaphor for what worry feels like — never state it as the word's literal origin.**

**καταμάθετε (katamathete) — G2648 (lexical form καταμανθάνω), Matthew 6:28, "consider."** Aorist Imperative Active. The kata- prefix intensifies manthanō ("learn") into "learn thoroughly / grasp conclusively by observing carefully" — a standard, uncontroversial compound-verb reading. Jesus's command is not a passing glance; it is "study this until you have it."

**κρίνον (krinon) — G2918, Matthew 6:28, "lily."** Accusative plural κρίνα in the verse. **Honesty note:** BibleHub/Strong's states this is "universally understood to denote the lily," but botanical/biblical-geography scholarship (BiblePlaces.com, Carta Jerusalem) shows real, live disagreement — the leading candidate among botanists of the Holy Land is the **crown anemone** (_Anemone coronaria_), with gladioli, poppies, and crown daisies also proposed; some treat _krinon_ as a generic term for "wildflower" rather than one species. **Report as "a Galilean wildflower — most likely the crown anemone; the exact species is genuinely uncertain," not a confident "lily."**

**βασιλεία (basileia) — G932, Matthew 6:33, "kingdom."** Accusative singular βασιλείαν. Standard dual semantic range: abstract "reign/rule" and concrete "realm/territory" — safe to use either sense contextually; both are uncontested NT lexicography.

**εἰρήνη (eirēnē) — G1515, Philippians 4:7, "peace."** Nominative singular. From a root meaning "to join/tie into a whole." The LXX regularly and consistently translates Hebrew _shalom_ with _eirēnē_, and Hebrew _shalom_'s "wholeness/completeness" sense carries into Paul's usage — a standard, well-supported point, safe to state directly.

**φρουρέω (phroureō) — G5432, Philippians 4:7, "will guard" (φρουρήσει, Future Indicative Active).** From _pro_ ("before") + _horaō_ ("to see") — "watch in advance." Thayer's lexicon (not just popular preaching) defines it explicitly as "to guard... **by a military guard**." The verb's only other NT use (2 Corinthians 11:32) is explicitly military — a governor posting sentries to catch Paul at Damascus. **The garrison/sentry reading is textually solid, verified against Thayer's own wording, not overstated.** Paul's own circumstance — writing under armed guard (see historical notes below) — is a real, sourceable irony; state it as "Paul, himself under guard, promises his readers a guard," not as a claim Paul consciously intended the wordplay (plausible, not provable).

**αὐτάρκης (autarkēs) — G842, Philippians 4:11, "content."** From autos ("self") + arkeō ("be sufficient") — "self-sufficient." _Autarkeia_ was a genuine, well-known technical term in Stoic/Cynic ethics for self-sufficient detachment from circumstance. Paul's redirection toward Christ-dependence ("I can do all things through Him who strengthens me," v.13, immediately following) rather than self-reliance is standard, responsible scholarship — safe to use as written.

## 3. Historical / cultural context (verified, sourced)

- **Sermon on the Mount audience:** Galilee, a mixed crowd of disciples and onlookers (Matt 5:1-2). Scholarly consensus situates the audience among Galilean day laborers and subsistence farmers under Roman-era economic pressure — a small wealthy elite, a majority living hand-to-mouth. "Daily bread" anxiety (Matt 6:11, 25-34) was literal for this audience, not an abstraction. Source: BYU Religious Studies Center, "Sociocultural Context of the Sermon on the Mount" (rsc.byu.edu).
- **Ravens as unclean birds:** confirmed — ravens are listed among forbidden birds in Leviticus 11:15 and Deuteronomy 14:14. God commanding a ritually unclean, scavenger bird to deliver a prophet's provision is a real, sourceable point, not homiletical flourish.
- **Galilean wildflowers:** anemones and other wildflowers bloom abundantly but briefly across Galilee's hills, roughly December-April, then vanish (BiblePlaces.com). Ovens of the period were genuinely fuel-poor; dried grass and wildflower stalks were a standard, documented fuel source thrown into clay ovens for quick heat (tips.translation.bible commentary on Matt 6:30) — the "grass...tomorrow thrown into the oven" image reflects an actual daily practice.
- **Solomon's dream at Gibeon:** dated c. 970-931 BC (early reign). Gibeon is called "the great high place" (1 Kings 3:4) because the tabernacle/bronze altar had been relocated there — this was _before_ the Jerusalem Temple existed, so sacrificing at Gibeon was not yet irregular. The "ask what I shall give you" form is a recognized ancient Near Eastern dream-oracle genre (A. Leo Oppenheim, 1956), and 1 Kings 3 is regularly read by scholars as employing this convention, partly as royal-legitimation narrative (TheTorah.com, Enter the Bible) — cite with "scholars have noted..." framing.
- **Philippians — date/occasion:** genuinely contested; state fairly. Traditional/majority view: Rome, c. AD 60-62 (Acts 28-era imprisonment). A significant scholarly strand argues an earlier Ephesian imprisonment, c. AD 55 (inferred from 2 Cor 1:8, not directly narrated in Acts). A minority view proposes Caesarea (Acts 23-26), c. AD 57-58. Do not present one as settled.
- **Philippi:** a Roman colony (colonial status granted 27 BC), heavily populated by veteran soldiers, built as a "mini-Rome." First church on European soil, founded Acts 16 (Lydia; the Philippian jailer) — a standard claim in NT introductions.
- **The "guard" irony is textual, not inferred:** Paul references his "chains/bonds" repeatedly within the letter (Phil 1:7, 1:13-14, 1:17), including that his imprisonment became known "throughout the whole imperial guard" (praetorium, 1:13) — if the Roman-imprisonment view holds, Paul was in daily contact with the very kind of guard _phroureō_ evokes.
- **Nehemiah 8:10:** setting is the square before the Water Gate, post-exilic Jerusalem. Ezra read the Law aloud from daybreak to noon; the people wept (conviction over covenant failure); Nehemiah, Ezra, and the Levites instructed them not to mourn but to celebrate, because the day was holy.

## 5. Verified stories

**Day A — Hudson Taylor, the half-crown and the gloves.** VERIFIED (direct primary fetch). Hull, England, c. 1851-52. Young Taylor, practicing dependence on God before his China call, gives his last coin (a half-crown) to a man begging him to pray for his dying wife and starving family of nine. The next morning a package arrives: a pair of kid gloves with a half-sovereign coin folded inside — four times what he gave. Citation: James Hudson Taylor, _A Retrospect_ (China Inland Mission), Ch. III, "Preparation for Service" — Project Gutenberg #26744 (gutenberg.org/ebooks/26744), full text fetched directly. Flag: Taylor's own aside, "400 per cent for twelve hours investment," must be used as his characteristic wry testimony, never as a give-to-get formula.

**Day B — David Brainerd's last diary entry.** VERIFIED WITH CAVEAT (strong cross-corroboration; direct fetch of the primary page was blocked by file truncation). Brainerd, 29, dying of tuberculosis at Jonathan Edwards's home in Northampton, writes his final diary entry Oct. 2, 1747, one week before his death: his soul "sweetly set on God," "sweetly disposed to commit all to him, even my dearest friends, my dearest flock." Citation: Jonathan Edwards, _The Life of David Brainerd_ (1749), diary entry "Lord's Day, Oct. 2" — Edwards's own editorial note confirms these are the last words Brainerd wrote or dictated. No material blessing attached — a life "brief as grass" ending in surrendered peace, not anxious striving.

**Day C — C.T. Studd gives away £29,000.** VERIFIED WITH CAVEAT (facts cross-corroborated; original 1933 biography access-restricted — direct fetch blocked by an archive.org lending restriction, so the primary text was not personally read; the quote below is consistently and correctly attributed across independent secondary sources, not personally verified against Grubb's page). 1887: Studd inherits £29,000, gives it entirely away (£5,000 each to Moody's Bible institute, Muller's orphan work, a London poor mission, the Salvation Army's India work; remainder to his fiancee). His reasoning, as consistently quoted: "If Jesus Christ be God and died for me, then no sacrifice can be too great for me to make for Him." Dies 1931 at Ibambi, Congo, age 70, of untreated gallstones — poverty and hardship, not wealth, followed the gift. Citation: Norman P. Grubb, _C.T. Studd: Cricketer and Pioneer_ (London: Religious Tract Society/Lutterworth Press, 1933). Explicitly anti-prosperity-gospel — the strongest available check on a transactional reading of "seek first...added unto you."

**Day B' — Corrie ten Boom, thanksgiving for the fleas.** VERIFIED (direct excerpt fetch). Ravensbrück, 1944. Corrie balks at thanking God for the fleas infesting Barracks 28; her sister Betsie insists on 1 Thessalonians 5:18, "give thanks in ALL circumstances." Weeks later they learn guards never entered the barracks to stop their forbidden evening Bible readings — "that place is crawling with fleas." Citation: Corrie ten Boom with John and Elizabeth Sherrill, _The Hiding Place_ (Chosen Books, 1971), Ch. 14, "The Blue Sweater" — verbatim excerpt fetched directly.

**Day A' — Bonhoeffer's Christmas 1944 poem, "By Gracious Powers."** VERIFIED WITH CAVEAT (scholarly-sourced translation; original English SCM edition access-restricted). Dec. 19, 1944, Bonhoeffer writes from a Gestapo cell to his fiancee: "By good forces wonderfully sheltered, we await confidently, what may come." Executed at Flossenburg, April 9, 1945 — the joy was never vindicated by rescue in this life. Citation: "Von guten Machten," letter to Maria von Wedemeyer; published in Eberhard Bethge, ed., _Widerstand und Ergebung_ (1951) / English _Letters and Papers from Prison_ (SCM Press, 1953). Pastoral note: do not soften or omit the execution — the illustration's power is that the rejoicing was not rescued.
