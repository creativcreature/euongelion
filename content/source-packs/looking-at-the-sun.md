## 2. Lexicon (verified 2026-08-14 against BibleHub's Strong's / Thayer's / BDB pages)

### ἀφοράω — _aphoraō_ — Strong's **G872** — Hebrews 12:2 (ἀφορῶντες, _aphorōntes_)

- **Strong's:** "to look away from all else at one object, to fix one's eyes upon"; "from
  apo and horao; to consider attentively."
- **Abbott-Smith / HELPS:** "properly, 'looking away from all else, to fix one's gaze
  upon.'"
- **Thayer's:** "to turn the eyes away from other things and fix them on something."
- **Parsing (BibleHub interlinear, Hebrews 12:2):** Verb — Present Participle Active —
  Nominative Masculine Plural. **Present** tense: a continuing action, not a single glance.
- **NT occurrences: 2 only** — Philippians 2:23 and Hebrews 12:2.
- **Drafting rule:** the force is _away-from-in-order-to-toward_. Do not describe it merely
  as "focus" — the prefix ἀπό (_apo_, "away from") is doing half the work.

### εὐπερίστατος — _euperistatos_ — Strong's **G2139** — Hebrews 12:1

- **Strong's:** "from eu and a derivative of a presumed compound of peri and histemi; well
  standing around, i.e. (a competitor) thwarting (a racer) in every direction."
- **Thayer's:** "skilfully surrounding i.e. besetting, namely, to prevent or retard
  running." Thayer adds: "Not found elsewhere."
- **Occurrence: hapax legomenon** — once in the NT, Hebrews 12:1, and not attested
  elsewhere in surviving Greek literature.
- **Parsing:** Adjective — Accusative Feminine Singular (εὐπερίστατον).
- **Drafting rule:** state the hapax status plainly. Do not claim a settled meaning the
  lexicons hedge on — Thayer's own gloss is a reconstruction from the parts.

### ὄγκος — _ogkos_ — Strong's **G3591** — Hebrews 12:1

- "bulk, mass, hence a weight, burden." Noun — Accusative Masculine Singular.
- **Drafting rule:** it is not a moral word. It is mass. The verse names weight and sin as
  two separate things.

### ὑπομονή — _hypomonē_ — Strong's **G5281** — Hebrews 12:1

- "endurance, steadfastness, patient waiting for; cheerful endurance, constancy."
- KJV renders it **"patience"**; BSB renders it **"endurance."**
- **Drafting rule:** the two English words are the same Greek word. Say so — it is the
  hinge of the whole reading.

### περισπάω — _perispaō_ — Strong's **G4049** — Luke 10:40 (περιεσπᾶτο, _periespato_)

- **Strong's:** "from peri and spao; to drag all around, i.e. (figuratively) to distract
  (with care)."
- **Thayer's:** "to draw around, to draw away, distract; passive metaphorically, to be
  driven about mentally, to be distracted"; "over-occupied, too busy, about a thing."
- **Occurrence: once in the NT** — Luke 10:40.
- **Parsing:** imperfect passive — a continuing state done _to_ her, not a choice she made
  once.
- **Drafting rule:** the passive is the point. Martha did not decide to be distracted.

### קָוָה — _qavah_ — Strong's **H6960** — Isaiah 40:31

- **Strong's/BDB:** "to bind together (perhaps by twisting), i.e. collect; (figuratively)
  to expect." BDB gloss: "gather (together), look, patiently, tarry, wait (for, on, upon)."
- 49 occurrences.
- **Drafting rule:** BDB presents the binding/twisting sense as the root from which the
  waiting sense developed — but the word "perhaps" is in the lexicon and must survive into
  the prose. Say the lexicons _connect_ it to twisting; do not assert the etymology as
  settled fact, and do not build the reading's argument on it.

## 3. Verified story — John Milton

**Verdict: VERIFIED.** Primary sources are the sonnet itself and Milton's own _Second
Defence_.

### Sonnet 19, complete, as printed in the John Milton Reading Room (Dartmouth), from _Poems_ (1673)

> When I consider how my light is spent,
> E're half my days, in this dark world and wide,
> And that one Talent which is death to hide,
> Lodg'd with me useless, though my Soul more bent
> To serve therewith my Maker, and present
> My true account, least he returning chide,
> Doth God exact day labour, light deny'd,
> I fondly ask; But patience to prevent
> That murmur, soon replies, God doth not need
> Either man's work or his own gifts, who best
> Bear his milde yoak, they serve him best, his State
> Is Kingly. Thousands at his bidding speed
> And post o're Land and Ocean without rest:
> They also serve who only stand and waite.

**Caveats that must be carried into the prose:**

- **The date is not settled.** Milton's blindness became complete in **1652**; the sonnet
  was first published in **1673**. Editors place composition somewhere between 1652 and
  1655 — the later date follows from the sonnets appearing in chronological order. Say
  "somewhere between 1652 and 1655," not a single year.
- **The title "On His Blindness" is not Milton's.** It was supplied by a later editor
  (Thomas Newton, eighteenth century). The 1673 volume numbers it only as a sonnet.
- Original spelling above is Milton's; if modernised in the prose, say so or quote only the
  final line, which is stable in every edition: **"They also serve who only stand and
  waite."**

### _Second Defence of the English People_ (1654), Robert Fellowes's English translation

Verified quotations:

- "It is not so wretched to be blind, as it is not to be capable of enduring blindness."
- On the physicians' warning before he wrote the first _Defence_: "when I had to contend
  with the pressure of sickness, and with the apprehension of soon losing the sight of my
  remaining eye, and when my medical attendants clearly announced, that if I did engage in
  the work, it would be irreparably lost" — and: "their premonitions caused no hesitation,
  and inspired no dismay."
- "mine keeps from my view only the coloured surfaces of things, while it leaves me at
  liberty to contemplate the beauty and stability of virtue and of truth."

**Citation caveat:** the _Second Defence_ was written in Latin (_Defensio Secunda pro
Populo Anglicano_, 1654). All English above is Fellowes's translation. Attribute it as a
translation, not as Milton's English.

## 4. Verified quote — Blaise Pascal

**Verdict: VERIFIED, with a paraphrase warning.**

- **Original French (Pensées, Lafuma 136 / Brunschvicg 139, the _divertissement_
  fragment):** "j'ai dit souvent que tout le malheur des hommes vient d'une seule chose,
  qui est de ne savoir pas demeurer en repos dans une chambre."
- **Faithful English:** "I have often said that all the unhappiness of men comes from one
  single thing, which is not knowing how to remain at rest in a room."
- **REJECTED as a quotation:** "All of humanity's problems stem from man's inability to sit
  quietly in a room alone." This is the version in circulation and it is a **paraphrase** —
  it adds "alone," which is not in the French, and it is not the wording of any standard
  translation. If the popular version is mentioned at all, it must be flagged as a
  paraphrase, exactly as it is in the drafted `insight` module.

## 7. Historical & cultural context

- **Isaiah 40** opens the second movement of the book, addressed to a people in or facing
  exile — "her forced labor has been completed" (40:2). The promise of renewed strength in
  40:31 is not spoken to the fresh. It is spoken to people who have already been used up,
  and its immediate contrast is that _even youths_ fall (40:30).
- **Hebrews 12:1** follows immediately on Hebrews 11's catalogue of the faithful, who
  "died in faith, without having received the things they were promised" (11:13). The
  "cloud of witnesses" is therefore not a stadium of spectators cheering a finish; it is a
  company of people who ran without seeing the end.
- **Luke 10:38-42** places Martha's house on the journey to Jerusalem. In Luke's ordering,
  it comes directly after the Good Samaritan — a parable about a man who stops to serve.
  The two stories sit side by side deliberately: Luke is not against service.
- **1 Kings 19** follows Elijah's greatest public success at Carmel. The collapse comes
  after the victory, not after a defeat.
- **Broom tree** (Hebrew _rothem_): a desert shrub of the Negev and Sinai, large enough to
  cast usable shade over a lying man. It is the only shade available where he stopped.

## 8. Christological connection

Hebrews 12:2 does not stop at "looking unto Jesus." It says _why_ the looking works: "who
for the joy that was set before him endured the cross." The one being looked at ran the
same way He is asking to be looked at — He endured by looking at something set before Him.
The instruction is not to imitate a technique but to fix on a Person who already did it,
and who is now "set down" — the only figure in the passage who is sitting.

Martha's arc completes the point: the woman corrected in Luke 10 is the woman who, in John
11:27, makes one of the plainest confessions of Christ in the Gospels — "I believe that You
are the Christ, the Son of God." Jesus' correction of Martha was never a dismissal of her.
