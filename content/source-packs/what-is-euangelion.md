## 2. VOCABULARY DATA (verified)

### εὐαγγέλιον (_euangelion_) — Greek

- **Strong's:** G2098
- **Transliteration:** _euangelion_
- **Pronunciation:** yoo-ang-ghel'-ee-on
- **Etymology:** Compound of _eu-_ (εὖ, "good, well") + _angelos_ (ἄγγελος, "messenger") via the noun-form derivative _angelia_ (ἀγγελία, "message, announcement"). Literally: "good message" or "good announcement."
- **Pre-Christian usage:** Used in the Greek-speaking Roman world for public proclamations of significant events: a battlefield victory, the birth of an heir to a Caesar, the accession of a new emperor. Famously, the Priene Calendar Inscription (~9 BC) refers to the birthday of Augustus as the beginning of "good news" (_euangelion_) for the world.
- **NT frequency:** 76 times across the New Testament; Mark uses it eight times in his short gospel.
- **First gospel use:** Mark 1:1, where Mark borrows the imperial-proclamation register and applies it to Jesus.

### בְּשׂוֹרָה (_besorah_) — Hebrew

- **Strong's:** H1309
- **Transliteration:** _besorah_
- **Pronunciation:** beh-sor-aw'
- **Etymology:** From the verb _basar_ (בָּשַׂר, H1319), "to bear tidings, announce."
- **OT usage:** Used for both good and bad news depending on context (2 Sam 4:10, 18:20, 22, 25, 27; 2 Kings 7:9). The cognate verb form is what Isaiah uses in 52:7, 40:9, 41:27, 60:6, and 61:1 — passages that form the Old Testament backbone for the New Testament gospel announcement.
- **Septuagint translation:** The Greek translators of the Hebrew Bible (the Septuagint, ~250 BC) chose forms of _euangelion / euangelizomai_ to render _besorah / basar_ — directly linking the two words in the bilingual Jewish world Jesus inhabited.

> _"Our wisdom, in so far as it ought to be deemed true and solid wisdom, consists almost entirely of two parts: the knowledge of God and of ourselves."_

**Why it fits Day 2:** Day 2 builds the historical and prophetic context — Isaiah's exiles waiting for a watchman's cry. ### Day 3 quote 1 — Athanasius

> _"God became man so that man might become god."_

_Citation: Athanasius of Alexandria, **On the Incarnation** (~318 AD), Section 54. (Note: "god" here refers to theosis/deification — being made fully alive in God's life — not ontological equality with God. The writer must define this in line so the Sleep-pathway reader does not misread.)_

**Why it fits Day 3:** Day 3 is the chiastic pivot — _the Messenger and the Message are the same person._ Athanasius states the structural claim of the incarnation in one sentence: God came in person, and that personal coming is what changes the human situation. The writer must accompany the quote with one sentence of context for a non-Christian reader (e.g., "Athanasius wrote this around 318 AD, when the church was still arguing about whether Jesus was actually God or merely sent by God").

**Why it fits Day 3:** Restates the same structural claim in different language. Pairs naturally with Athanasius — first the metaphysical claim ("God became man"), then the moral claim ("everything is already done"). The two together set up Day 4's "what receiving it looks like."

## 4. HISTORICAL CONTEXT (verified — for the writer agent's framing)

### Mark's gospel and the Roman world (Day 1)

- Mark wrote, by traditional reckoning, in Rome in the late 60s AD, possibly during or just after Nero's persecution.
- Eusebius (_Ecclesiastical History_, ~325 AD, Book 3) preserves an early tradition that Mark composed his account from Peter's preaching.
- The word _euangelion_ in the Greek-speaking Roman empire was, at the time Mark wrote, a public-square word — the kind of announcement a herald would carry from a battlefield victory or an imperial accession.
- Mark's opening line ("the beginning of the _euangelion_ of Jesus Christ, the Son of God") deliberately echoes imperial language. To call Jesus "Son of God" was, in 60s-AD Roman ears, a phrase that competed with how Caesar Augustus's birth had been announced (the **Priene Calendar Inscription**, ~9 BC, calls the birth of Augustus "the beginning of _euangelion_ for the world").
- Mark is therefore making a political claim before he makes a theological one. The good news is not new wisdom; it is the announcement that the true king has shown up.

### Isaiah 52:7 in its prophetic setting (Day 2)

- Isaiah 40–55 (the so-called "Book of Comfort") addresses a community of exiles — Israel deported to Babylon in 587 BC.
- The watchman's image in 52:7 is the runner who arrives ahead of the returning king with news that the king is coming home and the captivity is over.
- Paul quotes this verse directly in Romans 10:15. He is reading the prophet Christologically: the runner who arrives ahead is now any preacher; the king who is coming home is Jesus.

### 1 Corinthians 15 as the earliest creed (Day 3)

- 1 Corinthians was written ~AD 53–54.
- The four-line creedal formula in 15:3–5 ("died … buried … raised … appeared") is widely held by NT scholars to be an even earlier oral tradition, likely formulated within five years of the crucifixion. Paul says he "received" it — meaning it predates his own conversion-era teaching.
- This is the earliest fixed-form Christian summary of the gospel that survives.

### Romans 1:16–17 in the Reformation (Day 4)

- Luther traced his own theological breakthrough to wrestling with Romans 1:17 — specifically the phrase "the righteousness of God." His autobiographical preface to the 1545 Latin edition of his works describes the moment he understood "the righteousness of God" as righteousness _from_ God given as gift, not righteousness _demanded by_ God as standard.
- That single reading reframed his whole reading of the Bible and started the Reformation.

### Acts 8 and the Ethiopian eunuch (Day 5)

- The eunuch is a court official ("in charge of the entire treasury") of the Kandake of Cush — a region in modern-day Sudan, well south of Roman Egypt.
- He has been to Jerusalem "to worship," meaning he is at minimum a God-fearer, possibly a full convert (though as a eunuch he would have been formally restricted under Deuteronomy 23:1; Isaiah 56:3–5 explicitly extends the covenant to eunuchs, which is exactly the kind of passage he might be reading on the road home).
- He is reading Isaiah 53. Philip "began with this very Scripture and told him the good news about Jesus" (8:35). The passage of choice is the Servant Song — the very passage 1 Cor 15 says Jesus died according to.
- Tradition (Eusebius, Book 2) credits this eunuch with founding the Ethiopian church on his return — making him the first explicitly recorded African Christian.

### 2 Corinthians 5 as the Sabbath day's anchor (Day 7)

- Written ~AD 55–56.
- The phrase "ministry of reconciliation" (5:18) and "ambassadors for Christ" (5:20) compress the entire arc of the series into two metaphors: the message (reconciliation) and the messenger (ambassador). Sabbath day reads it without commentary.

## 5. NOTES FOR THE WRITER AGENT

1. **Translation discipline.** The brief specifies which translation each anchor uses. Do not substitute. The validator will fail the build if a `scripture` module declares a translation not in `[BSB, WEB, WEBBE, KJV, ASV, YLT, DARBY, BBE]`.
2. **Citation discipline.** Every external quote in this pack carries its full citation. When the prose includes one of these quotes, the day's `resource` module must contain the full citation in an `endnotes` or `sources` field.
3. **Sleep-pathway tone.** Define every theological term in line the first time it appears. _Grace_, _gospel_, _atonement_, _kerygma_, _propitiation_, _theosis_, _justification_ — never assume the reader knows these. The reader is intelligent but biblically untrained.
4. **No insider language.** Per [docs/PUBLIC-FACING-LANGUAGE.md](docs/PUBLIC-FACING-LANGUAGE.md): the prose never uses "spiritual journey," "incredible journey," "embark on a journey," etc. The validator regex catches these.
5. **The chiastic pivot is Day 3.** Write Day 3 first. Everything else mirrors it.
6. **Day 5 ends in invitation, not decision.** No sinner's prayer template. The Acts 8 narrative ends with the eunuch going on his way rejoicing — that is the pattern. The writer's closing prayer should be permissive, not coercive.
7. **Vocabulary modules carry weight.** _Euangelion_ on Day 1 and _besorah_ on Day 2 are the etymological backbone. The reader should leave Day 2 understanding that the gospel was a Hebrew concept before it was a Greek word.
