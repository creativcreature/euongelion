**Drafting note — the translation seam is teachable, not a problem to hide.**
KJV 6:27 reads "add one cubit unto his stature" (a measure of HEIGHT); BSB reads
"add a single hour to his life" (a measure of TIME); ASV splits the difference
with "one cubit unto the measure of his life." The Greek noun is _hēlikia_,
which genuinely carries both. Show the reader the seam. **Do not assert which is
correct beyond what the lexicon agent verified** — see §3.

### 3.1 μεριμνάω _merimnaō_ — G3309 (Day 2, the engine of the week)

- **Spelling:** μεριμνάω · **Translit:** merimnaō · **Phonetic:** mer-im-nah'-o · **Verb**
- **Strong's summary (verbatim):** "To be anxious, to worry, to care for"
- **Thayer's (verbatim):** "To be anxious; to be troubled with cares" and "to care
  for, look out for (a thing); to seek to promote one's interests"
- **HELPS Word-studies (verbatim):** "Properly, drawn in opposite directions;
  'divided into parts'…to go to pieces because pulled apart (in different
  directions), like the force exerted by sinful anxiety (worry)."
- **Origin:** from G3308 _merimna_ (worries). HELPS derives _merimna_ from "a
  part, as opposed to the whole."

**HONESTY NOTE — how far the "divided" claim may be pushed.** The pulled-apart
sense is HELPS Word-studies' reading, and it is a real lexicon, so it may be
attributed: _"one lexicon glosses it…"_. It may NOT be stated as though the word
transparently means "to divide," and it may NOT be traced directly to _merizō_.
The stated derivation is from _merimna_. Attribute the reading; do not upgrade it
to a bare fact.

- **19 NT occurrences:** Matt 6:25, 27, 28, 31, 34 (×2); Matt 10:19; Luke 10:41;
  Luke 12:11, 22, 25, 26; 1 Cor 7:32, 33, 34 (×2); **1 Cor 12:25**;
  **Phil 2:20**; **Phil 4:6**.

**THE DAY 2 ARGUMENT, and it is airtight.** The same verb Paul uses in "be
anxious for nothing" (Phil 4:6) he uses **approvingly** two chapters earlier of
Timothy — Phil 2:20, who will genuinely _merimnaō_ for the Philippians' welfare —
and again of the church's members caring for one another in 1 Cor 12:25. A verb
Paul praises in one breath is not a verb he criminalises in the next. Whatever
Philippians 4:6 forbids, it is not caring. And Luke 10:41 is Jesus saying the
word gently, to Martha, by name, twice — not as a rebuke shouted at a crowd.

### 3.2 ἡλικία _hēlikia_ — G2244 (Day 2, the translation seam)

- **Spelling:** ἡλικία · **Translit:** hēlikia · **Noun, feminine**
- **Thayer's:** carries BOTH "age, time of life" AND "stature"; glossed as
  "maturity (in years or size)". Three senses distinguished: (1) age/time of
  life, (2) adult age/maturity, (3) stature.
- **8 NT occurrences:** Matt 6:27; Luke 2:52; Luke 12:25; Luke 19:3; John 9:21;
  John 9:23; Eph 4:13; Heb 11:11.

**VERDICT: the seam is real and may be taught as such.** KJV "add one cubit unto
his stature" (height) and BSB "add a single hour to his life" (time) are both
defensible renderings of one word. Present it as a genuine ambiguity in the
Greek, not as one translation being wrong.

### 3.3 φρουρέω _phroureō_ — G5432 (Day 5, "shall keep your hearts")

- **Spelling:** φρουρέω · **Translit:** phroureō · **Verb**
- **Definition (verbatim):** "to guard, protect by a military guard, either in
  order to prevent hostile invasion, **or to keep inhabitants of a besieged city
  from flight**."
- From φρουρός _phrouros_, "a sentinel, guard" — military sentinels posted at
  gates and city walls.
- **4 NT occurrences:** 2 Cor 11:32 (a governor garrisons Damascus's gates to
  arrest Paul); Gal 3:23 (the Law as protective custody until Christ);
  **Phil 4:7**; 1 Pet 1:5 (God's power guarding believers).

**DRAFTING GOLD.** The second half of the definition is the day's whole point:
a garrison does not only keep enemies out, it keeps the city's own people from
running. The peace of God is posted around a heart partly to stop it fleeing.
This is verbatim lexicon, not homiletic invention — it may be taught directly.

### 3.4 ἐγγύς _engys_ — G1451 (Day 4, "The Lord is at hand")

- **Spelling:** ἐγγύς · **Translit:** engys · **Adverb** · "Near, close, at hand"
- **Spatial:** "of place and position" — John 19:42, Acts 1:12.
- **Temporal:** "of Time; concerning things imminent and soon to come to pass" —
  Matt 24:32, Mark 13:28, Luke 21:30-31, Rev 1:3, 22:10.
- Also Eph 2:13 (brought near by the blood) and Rom 10:8 (the word is near you).

**VERDICT: genuinely both, and Phil 4:5 does not disambiguate.** The day may hold
the ambiguity open — He is near in place and near in time — but must not claim
the Greek settles it.

### 3.5 אֱמוּנָה _emunah_ — H530 (Day 3, "how to faith", and the spine's "steadyness")

- **Spelling:** אֱמוּנָה · **Translit:** 'ĕmūnāh · **Noun, feminine**
- **Definition (verbatim):** "Faithfulness, fidelity, steadfastness, trust,
  faith," in three senses, in this order: **(1) "literally firmness"**,
  (2) "figuratively security", (3) "morally fidelity".
- ~49 occurrences. Key: **Exodus 17:12**, Deut 32:4, Ps 36:5, Ps 89, **Hab 2:4**.

**THE CENTRAL FIND OF THE WEEK.** The literal sense is _firmness_ — steadiness —
and it precedes any sense of belief. And the flagship occurrence is not abstract:

**Exodus 17:12, interlinear-verified word by word.** וַיְהִ֥י יָדָ֛יו אֱמוּנָ֖ה —
_way·hî yā·ḏāw 'ĕmūnāh_ — "and his hands were **steady**" (H530), Moses' arms
held up by Aaron and Hur, one on each side, until the going down of the sun.
The Bible's own root picture of _emunah_ is not a feeling generated in a chest.
It is a pair of tired arms that stayed up **because two men held them there**.
That is the answer to "how to faith," and it is exegesis, not metaphor.

Habakkuk 2:4 — "the righteous will live by his _emunah_" — is quoted three times
in the New Testament (Rom 1:17; Gal 3:11; Heb 10:38).

### 3.6 צוּר _tsur_ — H6697 (Day 4, the Rock)

- **Spelling:** צוּר · **Translit:** tsur · **Noun, masculine**
- **Definition:** "Rock, Cliff, Boulder" — four senses: a cliff or sharp rock
  (compressed formation); a rock or boulder; **a refuge (figurative)**; an edge.
- **33 occurrences** use "Rock" of God figuratively, emphasising "support and
  defence of his people."
- Key: Deut 32:4; Ps 18:2 (via 2 Sam 22:3, "my rock, in whom I take refuge").

**CAVEAT — do not assert a tsur/sela contrast.** The Strong's page fetched does
NOT compare _tsur_ with _sela_ (H5553). No distinction between them was verified,
so **no such distinction may be drawn in the draft.** Cut the comparison; teach
_tsur_ alone.

### 3.7 THE JOIN — Deuteronomy 32:4 carries BOTH words (interlinear-verified)

Word-by-word confirmed: הַצּוּר _haṣ·ṣūr_ = **H6697** ("The Rock") and
אֱמוּנָה _'ĕmūnāh_ = **H530** ("faithfulness"), in the same verse. Strong's
numbers present in Deut 32:4 include 6697, 8549, 6467, 3605, 1870, 4941, 410,
530, 369, 5766, 6662, 3477.

> "He is the Rock, His work is perfect; all His ways are just. A God of
> faithfulness without injustice, righteous and upright is He." (BSB)

**This is the structural hinge of the series.** The founder's spine names "Him
Being a Rock" and "standing deeply on Faith / His steadyness" as separate
clauses; Moses put them in one sentence. Day 3 (_emunah_ — our steadiness held
up) and Day 4 (_tsur_ — His immovability) are therefore two halves of one verse,
and Day 4 should say so out loud, closing the chiasm's centre back on itself.

### The Silence translation ruling (this is the day's best content)

Johnston's English is IMPERATIVE — "Trample! Trample!" — but **Endō's Japanese is
PERMISSIVE**. This is documented, not lore: Van C. Gessel (_Christianity and
Literature_ 48, 1999), Mase-Hasegawa's rendering ("You may trample. You may
trample."), and Endō's widow **Junko Endō**, who reported being shocked that the
English and French editions used the imperative mood. **Ship the published
Johnston wording, cite it, and add the permissive note** — the mood difference IS
the theology. An imperative makes God the author of the man's failure; a
permission makes Him the one who absorbs it.

## 5b. APOCRYPHA KILLED — documented, never used

1. **"Worry is like a rocking chair…"** — APOCRYPHAL for BOTH Corrie ten Boom and
   Erma Bombeck. Traced to the _Milford Chronicle_ (Milford, Delaware),
   **26 March 1948, p. 17**, an unsigned filler in a humour column bylined "Billy
   the Goat." Bombeck was 21 and unsyndicated until 1965; ten Boom did not publish
   in English until 1954. Library of Congress full-text hits run 1948-1963 across
   five states, never once attributed to a named author.
2. **Luther, "Pray, and let God worry"** — DISPUTED wording, REAL source. The
   letter to Catharine Luther, Eisleben, 10 Feb 1546 (eight days before his
   death) reads "Pray, and let God take thought," citing Psalm 55. Ship the
   letter or nothing; never the bare aphorism.
3. **Augustine "our hearts are restless"** — REAL (_Confessions_ I.i.1), but the
   popular English form matches NO single translation. Name the translator if
   used (Pusey 1838 or Outler 1955).
4. Plus the four killed by the stories agent: Luther's "Here I stand" (famous
   clause only), the "People are often unreasonable" text attributed to Mother
   Teresa (on the Missionaries of Charity's OWN false-attribution register —
   actually Kent M. Keith), Francis of Assisi's "preach the gospel… use words"
   (0 grep hits in the complete _Writings_), and a Brainerd/Jerusha Edwards
   betrothal (0 hits for betroth/espous/courtship in Edwards' memoir).

## 7. Standing drafting rules for this series

1. **The reader who is actually anxious is the reader.** Name the misuse of these
   verses once, early, on Day 2 — then teach. No running polemic (founder ruling).
2. **Never wrap a quotation inside a longer sentence** (SA-053). Setup sentence.
   Quote as its own sentence. Response sentence. This is the single largest
   source of density in the catalog.
3. **Never write "devotional" in reader-facing prose** — the validator scans for
   it as a forbidden label. Use "reading" or "this series."
4. **Hold the tension on Day 3.** Do not resolve God's silence in three
   paragraphs (spec §9.9). The honest answer is Habakkuk's: wait for it.
5. **"All these things" means food, drink and clothing.** The passage defines its
   own phrase in v31. Any drift toward a prosperity reading is a failure of the
   series' central claim, and Day 1 should close that door explicitly and once.
