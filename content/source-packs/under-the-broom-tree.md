## 3. Historical/cultural context (verified, sourced)

- **Dates:** No single fixed date for Ahab's reign; standard chronologies cluster mid-9th century BC (Thiele: 874–853 BC; Albright: 869–850 BC; Coogan: 871–852 BC). One externally fixed anchor: Ahab is independently attested fighting Shalmaneser III of Assyria at the Battle of Qarqar, 853 BC (Assyrian records). Honest phrasing: "most likely the 870s–850s BC."
- **Jezebel:** Daughter of Ethbaal (Ittobaal I), priest-king of Tyre and Sidon; her marriage to Ahab sealed a Tyre–Israel alliance. She promoted Baal-Melqart and Asherah worship, sponsoring state-funded prophetic guilds (450 prophets of Baal, 400 of Asherah, per 1 Kings 18:19).
- **Beersheba:** The traditional southernmost point of settled Israel/Judah — half of the merism "from Dan to Beersheba" (used 9x in the Hebrew Bible for the whole land). Fleeing there puts Elijah at the farthest habitable edge of the kingdom from Jezreel before he pushes into true wilderness.
- **Forty days and nights:** Widely recognized as a deliberate echo of Moses — who fasted 40 days on this same mountain receiving the covenant (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 9:9). Scholars hold both the literal itinerary and the typological echo together rather than choosing one; a Moses→Elijah→Jesus (wilderness, 40 days) chain is a standard homiletical/scholarly observation.
- **Horeb = Sinai:** Standard scholarly position treats them as the same mountain (name distribution tracks source-critical strands). Exact location is genuinely unmarked/debated — the traditional Jebel Musa identification is a later (Byzantine-era) tradition, not a biblically fixed location. Phrase accordingly: "traditionally identified with a peak in the southern Sinai Peninsula, but the identification is tradition, not a fixed biblical claim."
- **Rabbinic tradition (labeled as tradition, not fact):** Rabbi Jonathan Sacks' _Covenant & Conversation_ essay on this passage, drawing on Midrash, reads the repeated question as a rebuke — that Elijah, as a prophet, should have interceded for the people rather than only accused them (contrast Moses at the golden calf, who pleaded for Israel). Cited here as a labeled rabbinic/modern-restatement tradition, not as the text's own claim.

## 4. Stories (verified per day; primary sources fetched directly except where noted)

### Day 2 — Charles Spurgeon's own depression (VERIFIED, primary text read in full)

Source: _Lectures to My Students_, First Series, Lecture 11, "The Minister's Fainting Fits" (1875). Verified quote (p. 123): "Fits of depression come over the most of us. Usually cheerful as we may be, we must at intervals be cast down. The strong are not always vigorous, the wise not always ready, the brave not always courageous, and the joyous not always happy... Knowing by most painful experience what deep depression of spirit means, being visited therewith at seasons by no means few or far between, I thought it might be consolatory to some of my brethren if I gave my thoughts thereon, that younger men might not fancy that some strange thing had happened to them when they became for a season possessed by melancholy." Verified quote (p. 129, on the Surrey Gardens disaster specifically): "To the lot of few does it fall to pass through such a horror of great darkness as that which fell upon me after the deplorable accident at the Surrey Music Hall. I was pressed beyond measure and out of bounds with an enormous weight of misery. The tumult, the panic, the deaths, were day and night before me, and made life a burden... From that dream of horror I was awakened in a moment by the gracious application to my soul of the text, 'Him hath God the Father exalted.'" The Surrey Gardens Music Hall stampede (false cry of "Fire," 7 dead, 28 injured) occurred October 19, 1856, during a Spurgeon service, at the peak of his early fame (age 22, already the most talked-about preacher in London). He names the darkness that followed as the worst depression of his life, and separately links depressive collapse generally to "the hour of great success." Folklore to avoid: circulated blog "quotes" attributing explicit suicidal language to this lecture are not in the primary text — do not use them.

### Day 3 — John Newton's care for William Cowper, January 1773 (verified via secondary scholarly citation of Newton's diary, not independently accessed in manuscript)

Cowper suffered a psychotic depressive break convinced God commanded his suicide; Mary Unwin sent for Newton in the early hours. Newton's diary, as quoted in scholarly secondary sources (Center for Preaching and Pastoral Leadership; Desiring God, "A Friend in the Fire," cross-checked by direct WebSearch against the same quoted lines): "I was sent for early this morning and returned astonished and grieved" (Jan 2, 1773); "I have now devoted myself and time as much as possible to attend on Mr. Cowper" (Jan 5); called again "at 4 in the morning" during a further crisis (Jan 24). Newton moved Cowper and Unwin into his own home for over a year afterward. Caveat carried into the draft: this is diary material verified through direct scholarly quotation, not a primary-manuscript read — cite it as such. Do NOT use the claimed "Amazing Grace" dating connection (under-verified, excluded).

### Day 4 — Blaise Pascal's Memorial, Nov 23 1654 (VERIFIED, primary translation fetched directly from Project Gutenberg)

Source: _The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal_, trans. C. Kegan Paul (Project Gutenberg ebook #46921). Verified opening: "This year of Grace 1654, Monday, November 23rd... FIRE. God of Abraham, God of Isaac, God of Jacob, Not of the philosophers and the wise." Verified closing register includes "Joy, joy, joy, tears of joy" and "Renunciation total and sweet." Sewn into his coat lining; discovered by a servant sorting his clothes after his death in 1662. No public event, no dramatized vision — a private, dated, terse nighttime note. Folklore to avoid: popular retellings dramatize this into an ecstatic "night of fire" vision with invented sensory detail; the real document is famously spare. Do not borrow those embellishments.

### Day 5 — Charles Colson and Prison Fellowship (VERIFIED via memoir, interview, organizational record)

Sources: _Born Again_ (1976); _Christianity Today_, "Why Charles Colson's Heart Is Still in Prison" (1983); Prison Fellowship organizational history. Colson entered Maxwell Federal Prison Camp in 1974 after the Watergate collapse — total public disgrace. Founded Prison Fellowship in 1976, built explicitly around training volunteers and inmates themselves to lead studies, rather than centering the ministry on his own presence; verified quote, asked who should carry it forward: "Anybody who wants it!" Grew from 6 staff/$90,000 (1976) to ~170 staff/15,000 volunteers within seven years.

### Day 6 — Brother Andrew and isolated congregations behind the Iron Curtain (VERIFIED facts and dates; structural fit noted honestly)

Source: _God's Smuggler_ (1967, with John and Elizabeth Sherrill); Open Doors organizational history. From a 1955 Warsaw trip onward, over 15 years of repeated visits, Brother Andrew met isolated Christian congregations behind the Iron Curtain who told him directly they believed the global church had forgotten them entirely — each one wrong in the same way, independently, again and again. Framing note: Andrew is the outside messenger, not the isolated person himself, so the story illustrates the _frequency and universality_ of Elijah's exact false belief across many real, documented people rather than mirroring his personal arc one-for-one — the draft will frame it that way rather than overclaiming a tighter parallel. Folklore to avoid: the popularized "one daring border crossing" version flattens a 15-year relational process; don't use it.

This is distinct from — and should not be conflated with — the separately-reported (and NOT used in this build) claim that Newton's Jan 1, 1773 "Amazing Grace" sermon coincided with Cowper's crisis; that dating link remains under-verified per the original story-research agent and stays excluded.
