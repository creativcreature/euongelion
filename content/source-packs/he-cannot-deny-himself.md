## 2. Lexicon — VERIFIED 2026-08-10

**Read the downgrade list before writing a single vocab module.** Five of the claims this series was built on are outright factual errors, and three of them were day-payoffs. The exact pointed forms and Strong's numbers for the twelve vocab modules are in the data table at §2.4; nothing may be printed that is not in it.

### 2.1 The three findings worth keeping above everything else

**(1) Genesis 15's silence is the point — and it makes Day 5 stronger, not weaker.**
The two elements the standard sermon fuses — _passing between the halves_ and _invoking a self-curse_ — are attested in the extrabiblical evidence **separately and never together**. Passing-between appears in a Hittite **purification** rite (KUB XVII 28 iv 45–56, for the lustration of a defeated army; Masson, _RHR_ 137/1 [1950]: 5–25 — explicitly purification, not covenant). Self-cursing over a cut animal appears in first-millennium Syrian/Assyrian curse formulas (Sefire KAI 222; Ashurnirari V, SAA 2 no. 2) **without** passing-between. The only place the two are joined is the Hebrew Bible — Jeremiah 34, and by inference Genesis 15.

Every extrabiblical maledictory rite curses the **inferior** party and says the curse **out loud**. Genesis 15 has the **superior** party move through the pieces and contains **no curse language at all**. Petersen: _"we know of no covenant in which the obviously superior party, much less a deity, is put under a potential curse."_ **The silence is not a gap to be filled in with Mari.**

**(2) Day 3's buried treasure.** Numbers 23:19 says God does not **נָחַם** _nacham_ (H5162). Hosea 11:8 says God's **נִחוּמַי** _nichumay_ (H5150, derived from H5162) are kindled. Same three consonants, opposite directions — and _nichum_ occurs only **three times** in the whole Old Testament. The two texts the pivot sets side by side are built from the same root. **Name it as a tension; do not resolve it cheaply.**

**(3) Two points that hold with no hedge at all.** _Nechmad_ (Genesis 3:6) and _tachmod_ (Exodus 20:17) are the **same lemma, H2530** — the tree was the thing you must not covet. And _hesed_ in Hosea 6:4 and 6:6 is the **same word, H2617** — what their loyalty failed to be is exactly what God asked for. Both are clean and checkable.

### 2.3 TIER 2 — true but overstated (ship the honest phrasing)

- **_arum_ / _arummim_** — the sound-play is **real and deliberate** (Cassuto's vocalization argument is genuinely strong; Alter calls it a pun), but a **shared root is not established** and the theological payload is contested — Zvi Ron judges the proposed readings "not particularly profound." Write it as a sound-play the author clearly intended; do not claim one root.
- **_derek_** — the lexicons say "a road **as trodden**," not "worn down by habit." **Trim the repetition idea.**
- **_hesed_ = "covenant loyalty"** — majority-ish but genuinely contested; Stoebe and Zobel reject the legal-obligation element. Routledge opens: "opinion is divided over its meaning."
- **_rachamim_ from the word for womb** — BDB _proposes_ it with visible hedging. Say "share the same consonants, and many scholars think that is not an accident."
- **_kachash_ = "leanness" as well as "lies"** — rests on **one verse** (Job 16:8) and is an etymological hypothesis. Use Cambridge's better-attested **"to lie = to disappoint"** instead.
- **"Peace and security" as a Roman imperial slogan** — **genuinely disputed** (Koester/Wengst/Weima for; Joel R. White, _NTS_ 59.3 [2013], against; only two pre-Pauline occurrences, and no classical commentator mentions Rome). **Use the Ezekiel 13:10 / Jeremiah 6:14 false-peace echo instead** — far better attested, and the link the tradition actually makes.
- **_asphaleia_ as "no-slip"** — the etymology (a-privative + _sphallō_) is sound, but by the Koine the word simply **means** safety/certainty; Luke 1:4 uses it for the "certainty" of teaching with no stumbling imagery. **Barr's etymological fallacy.** Background only — never "what Paul means."
- **_harpagmos_** — a **hapax** whose sense is unsettled, and **Hoover's now-dominant reading ("something to take advantage of") REVERSES the shape of the Adam contrast**: on it, Christ _had_ equality and declined to exploit it. **Pick a reading and name the other** — KJV and modern readers are looking at materially different sentences.
- **Hebrews 8:8–12 "quotes Jeremiah verbatim"** — say **"quotes at length, following the Greek."** It is the **longest continuous** OT quotation in the NT (verified: 135 words vs. Acts 2:17–21 at 108; the Romans rivals are catenae, not continuous), but it follows the **LXX** and diverges from the Hebrew at exactly the ἠμέλησα / _ba'alti_ point.
- **Jeremiah 31:31–34 written in the ruins in 586** — defensible (Lundbom, Holladay) but **not a datum**; Carroll, Thiel, Römer and Albertz place it later. **Preach the setting, not a year.**
- **Lamentations 3:22 "his mercies never cease"** — that is the **variant** (LXX, Peshitta, Targum, many medieval Hebrew MSS). **The MT reads "_we_ are not consumed."** K&D defend the MT; Cambridge and Pulpit prefer the variant. This is why translations disagree, and it is worth telling the reader.
- **2 Timothy 2:11–13 is a hymn** — widely held, **not unanimous**.
- **Pastoral authorship percentages** — say _"a clear majority of critical scholarship,"_ never a figure; note **2 Timothy is the one of the three most often allowed to be genuine.**
- **Hosea 6:1–3 as glib repentance God rejects** — majority in the older tradition, **not settled**; a serious line reads the people as sincere but not orthoprax. Either way **v.4 is grief, not verdict** — which is the devotionally load-bearing point and survives both readings.
- **"Sargon II destroyed Samaria"** — 2 Kings credits **Shalmaneser V**; Sargon's own inscriptions claim it. Write: _"Samaria fell to Assyria in 722/721; the sources disagree over which king took the city."_
- **"Shalman" at Hosea 10:14** — **unidentified**; "the identity and details of this event have been lost to history" (Tully). The rhetoric works without the referent.
- **2 Corinthians 3:6 quotes Jeremiah** — it **echoes**, and Ezekiel 36 is at least as much in the mix.
- **Qumran awaited Jeremiah's new covenant** — **CD never cites Jer 31**, and its "new covenant" means _more_ law and _more_ mediation, close to the inverse of Jer 31:34. Fair as shared terminology; overstated as shared theology. **Not used in this series.**

### 2.4a Two clauses stated exactly

**Ezekiel 36:27** — וְעָשִׂ֗יתִי אֵ֣ת אֲשֶׁר־בְּחֻקַּי֙ תֵּלֵ֔כוּ
Literal word order: _"and-I-will-do [obj.] that-which — in-my-statutes you(pl)-shall-walk"_ — i.e. **"and I will bring about that which — in my statutes you shall walk."** Both verbs are Qal (עָשִׂיתִי Qal conj-perf 1cs; תֵּלֵכוּ Qal imperf 2mp). The result-relation lives in the אֲשֶׁר clause: **God performs the action, and their walking is what that action produces.** That is accurate and may be written — the word "causative" may not.

**Lamentations 3:22** — the MT reads consonantal **תמנו**, vocalized _tamnu_, Qal perfect **1st common plural** of תָּמַם (H8552): _"**we** are not consumed."_ The variant reads **תמו**, _tammu_, Qal perfect **3rd common plural** of the same root, making "the LORD's mercies" the subject: _"they never cease."_ **The two differ by a single letter — the nun.** The shorter reading is supported by the LXX, Peshitta, Targum and many medieval Hebrew manuscripts, which is why translations diverge here. One sentence to the reader; keep it out of the sabbath prose and put it in the resource module.

### 2.5 A circularity to stand inside honestly

Hasel's "no self-curse in Genesis 15" case leans on comparing it to **second-millennium** texts rather than first-millennium ones — legitimate only if Genesis 15 is early. But Genesis 15's date is itself contested (Van Seters; J. Ha, _Genesis 15_, BZAW 181 [1989] place it exilic/post-exilic, which would make the maledictory parallels the _more_ apt ones). **The argument runs in a circle in both directions and neither side can close it. Say so rather than pick a side.**

Also worth knowing, and worth one honest line if Hebrews 8:13 is quoted: its obsolescence argument is derived **from the word "new" in the prophecy itself**, not from an event — and Goshen-Gottstein calls it "a clearly supersessionist context." It is the single most contested verse in Jewish–Christian reception of this passage. Relatedly: Jeremiah 31:31–34 gets **no mention in either Talmud** and one tannaitic citation (Sifra on Leviticus 26). **Its centrality is a Christian phenomenon** — a fact the series should carry lightly and honestly rather than ignore.

### Day 1 — Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910), _A Confession_, ch. IV

Verified first-person, sustained throughout; Aylmer Maude's translation of Tolstoy's own _Ispoved'_.

> It had come to this, that I, a healthy, fortunate man, felt I could no longer live: some irresistible power impelled me to rid myself one way or other of life. I cannot say I wished to kill myself. The power which drew me away from life was stronger, fuller, and more widespread than any mere wish… And it was then that I, a man favoured by fortune, hid a cord from myself, lest I should hang myself from the crosspiece of the partition in my room, where I undressed alone every evening; and I ceased to go out shooting with a gun, lest I should be tempted by so easy a way of ending my life. I did not myself know what I wanted: I feared life, desired to escape from it, yet still hoped something of it.

> And all this befell me at a time when all around me I had what is considered complete good fortune. I was not yet fifty; I had a good wife who loved me and whom I loved, good children, and a large estate which without much effort on my part improved and increased.

> "Very well; you will be more famous than Gogol or Pushkin or Shakespeare or Molière, or than all the writers in the world — and what of it?" And I could find no reply at all. (ch. III–IV)

**Citation:** Leo Tolstoy, _A Confession_, trans. Aylmer Maude, in _A Confession and What I Believe_, The World's Classics (Oxford University Press, 1921), ch. IV, pp. 21–22. Fetched: `archive.org/download/confessionwhatib00tols` (authoritative) and the Standard Ebooks transcription of the same Maude text (independent cross-check — agrees word-for-word).

**CAVEATS TO SHIP:**

### Day 2 — Augustine (354–430), the pear theft, _Confessions_ II.iv.9 and II.vi.12–14

> Yet I lusted to thieve, and did it, compelled by no hunger, nor poverty, but through a cloyedness of well-doing… For I stole that, of which I had enough, and much better. Nor cared I to enjoy what I stole, but joyed in the theft and sin itself. A pear tree there was near our vineyard, laden with fruit, tempting neither for colour nor taste. To shake and rob this, some lewd young fellows of us went, late one night (having according to our pestilent custom prolonged our sports in the streets till then), and took huge loads, not for our eating, but to fling to the very hogs, having only tasted them. And this, but to do what we liked only, because it was misliked… It was foul, and I loved it; I loved to perish, I loved mine own fault. (Pusey, II.iv.9)

> What then did wretched I so love in thee, thou theft of mine, thou deed of darkness, in that sixteenth year of my age?… Fair were those pears, but not them did my wretched soul desire; for I had store of better, and those I gathered, only that I might steal. (Pusey, II.vi.12)

> …Did I wish even by stealth to do contrary to Thy law, because by power I could not, so that being a prisoner, I might mimic a maimed liberty by doing with impunity things unpermitted me, a darkened likeness of Thy Omnipotency? (Pusey, II.vi.14)

Outler's modern English is also verified and may be used instead — "It was foul, and I loved it. I loved my own undoing," and "a sort of counterfeit liberty… in a deluded sense of omnipotence."

**Citation:** Pusey (Library of Fathers, 1838) via `ccel.org/ccel/a/augustine/confess`; Outler (Library of Christian Classics vol. VII, Westminster, 1955) via `ccel.org/ccel/a/augustine/confessions`. Cite as "Book II, ch. IV (§9)" — safe across both numbering systems.

**CAVEATS TO SHIP:**

- Augustine gives **his age, not the year**. "In that sixteenth year of my age" is his; "c. AD 370" is a modern calculation from his birth on 13 November 354. If a year is printed, mark it as a reckoning.
- This is a bishop in his forties interpreting his own adolescence through thirty years of later theology. That is the point — but the reader should know it is retrospective interpretation, not a diary.
- **Do not quote Chadwick** — unverifiable (see §4).

### Day 3 — William Cowper (1731–1800)

**The popular version of this story is false, and the evidence runs the opposite way.**

_The coveted post, in his own words:_

> I one day said to a friend of mine, "If the clerk of the journals of the House of Lords should die, I had some hopes that my kinsman, who had the place in his disposal, would appoint me to succeed him." We both agreed, that the business of that place being transacted in private, would exactly suit me; and both expressed an earnest wish for his death, that I might be provided for. Thus did I covet, what God had commanded me not to covet, and involved myself in still deeper guilt, by doing it in the spirit of a murderer.

_His own date for the darkness:_

> Conviction of sin, and expectation of instant judgment, never left me, from the 7th of December 1763, until the middle of July following.

_The turn — Romans 3:25, exactly as suspected:_

> I flung myself into a chair near the window, and, seeing a Bible there, ventured once more to apply to it for comfort and instruction. The first verse I saw was the 25th of the 3rd of Romans; "Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God." Immediately I received strength to believe it, and the full beams of the Sun of Righteousness shone upon me… Unless the Almighty arm had been under me, I think I should have died with gratitude and joy.

_John Newton on the friendship, from his abandoned memoir (quoted by Hayley) — the Day 3 line:_

> For nearly twelve years we were seldom separated for seven hours at a time, when we were awake, and at home:— The first six I passed in daily admiring, and aiming to imitate him: during the second six, I walked pensively with him in the valley of the shadow of death.

_Cowper, eleven years into the second darkness (to Newton, 13 January 1784):_

> If I am recoverable, why am I thus?… I forestall the answer — God's ways are mysterious, and he giveth no account of his matters… There is a mystery in my destruction, and in time it shall be explained.

_His last original poem, "The Castaway," dated 20 March 1799 — the conviction intact to the end:_

> No voice divine the storm allay'd, / No light propitious shone; / When, snatch'd from all effectual aid, / We perish'd, each alone: / But I beneath a rougher sea, / And whelm'd in deeper gulfs than he.

**Verified facts:** the 1763 crisis over the Clerk of the Journals; suicide attempts in 1763 and October 1773; St Albans under **Dr Nathaniel Cotton**; left St Albans **17 June 1765**; relapse **January 1773** into a permanent conviction of reprobation lasting 27 years; he wrote "God Moves in a Mysterious Way" (_Olney Hymns_, Book III, Hymn 15, signed "C."); Cowper lived in Newton's vicarage 12 April 1773 – 28 May 1774.

**CAVEATS TO SHIP — binding:**

- **NEVER say the hymn was written after a suicide attempt.** The attempt was October 1773; the hymn sits in a manuscript volume between a letter dated 4 November 1772 and a hymn marked 1773. Julian: "This supports the conclusion… that the hymn was not the outcome of his attempted suicide in October 1773." Greatheed, preaching Cowper's funeral three weeks after his death to people who knew him, says it was composed "during a solitary walk in the fields" in **presentiment** of the coming illness. If the timing is mentioned at all, say it was written **before** the darkness fell.
- **Call it the _Memoir of the Early Life_, not _Adelphi_.** They are distinct documents; _Adelphi_ is Cowper's account of his brother John's death (1770).
- The _Memoir_ is genuinely his own words but was **published against his express wishes** (1816) and printed editions are edited — Grimshawe openly suppressed "those portions which violate the feelings" and is arguing a thesis. The scholarly text is King & Ryskamp (Oxford, 1979–86).
- **Do not date the 1773 "fatal dream"** precisely — sources conflict (New Year's night vs. February 1773).
- Suicide content: attempts are attested; no method detail is needed and none is given. The theological weight is that the conviction of reprobation **never lifted** — and the hymns kept coming.

### Day 4 — Thomas Cranmer (1489–1556)

**The founder's key question is answered, and the answer is the best thing about this day: the hand in the fire does NOT rest on Foxe.**

The earliest surviving witness is **"J.A.," a Roman Catholic bystander writing two days after the burning**, who calls it "so evil a cause," dispraises Cranmer's "obstinate stubbornness" — and reports the gesture anyway:

> Fire being now put to him, he stretched out his right hand, and thrust it into the flame, and held it there a good space, before the fire came to any other part of his body; where his hand was seen of every man sensibly burning, crying with a loud voice, "This hand hath offended." As soon as the fire got up, he was very soon dead, never stirring or crying all the while.

Foxe's account of the same moment, for comparison:

> And when the wood was kindled, and the fire began to burn near him, stretching out his arm, he put his right hand into the flame, which he held so steadfast and immovable (saving that once with the same hand he wiped his face), that all men might see his hand burned before his body was touched… and oftentimes he repeated "his unworthy right hand," so long as his voice would suffer him.

**They agree** on the tears through Cole's sermon, the prayer, the creed, the full repudiation, the declaration that the hand would burn first, Ely of Brasenose refusing his hand, the two Spanish friars, his being silenced mid-sentence, and the hand held in the flame. **They diverge on the words at the stake:** "This hand hath offended" is the eyewitness's; "his unworthy right hand" is Foxe's. **Neither source has both** — do not merge them.

_Cranmer's own words at St Mary's (eyewitness version):_

> And now I come to the great thing that troubleth my conscience more than any other thing that ever I said or did in my life: and that is, the setting abroad of writings contrary to the truth. Which here now I renounce, and refuse, as things written with my hand contrary to the truth which I thought in my heart, and writ for fear of death, and to save my life, if it might be… And forasmuch as my hand offended in writing contrary to my heart, therefore my hand shall first be punished. For if I may come to the fire, it shall be first burned.

> Alas, my lord, I have been a man, that all my life loved plainness, and never dissembled till now against the truth; which I am most sorry for.

_His prayer, kneeling, before the speech:_

> O Lord, my sins be great, but yet have mercy upon me for thy great mercy… Thou didst not give thy Son, O Heavenly Father, for our little and small sins only, but for all and the greatest of the world, so that the sinner return and repent unto thee with his whole heart, as I do here at this present.

**Verified facts:** **SIX** recantations (MacCulloch, and the 1556 Cawood pamphlet), signed over roughly seven weeks; the **fifth** (26 Feb 1556) was the substantive one, drafted in Latin, most likely by the Spanish friar Villagarcía; degradation 14 February 1556 at Christ Church; Cole's sermon at St Mary's, 21 March 1556, moved indoors for weather; burned in Broad Street opposite Balliol, the same spot as Latimer and Ridley five months earlier (16 October 1555).

**CAVEATS TO SHIP:**

- **Say "six recantations," attributed to MacCulloch.** Not five. And **do not say "Foxe numbered them"** — Foxe printed exactly ONE (the fifth) in every lifetime edition. The numbered set is the Bonner/Cawood 1556 pamphlet.
- **Do NOT ship "he refused to sign a seventh."** Contested — Strype and Cattley assert it; Pollard rebuts it directly; MacCulloch does not endorse it.
- On Foxe's reliability, ship Patrick Collinson's judgement rather than a bare "Foxe is biased": "Foxe never made anything up… His history was not dispassionate and disinterested but highly partisan and polemical… But Foxe was not a liar, and, in the tradition of ecclesiastical history, he stuck more faithfully, even slavishly, to the documented record than many modern historians of some fame."
- Nichols (1859) argues Foxe's account was _derived from_ the J.A. letter — flagged by Nichols himself as "I am inclined to think." Either way the earliest witness is the hostile bystander.
- **Series-wide trap:** "Play the man, Master Ridley" first appears in Foxe's **1570** edition and the Acts and Monuments Online editors say he "may just possibly have invented" it. Not used here; flagged for future builds.

**Citations:** Letter of "J.A.", BL MS Harleian 422, dated 23 March 1555/6, printed in H. J. Todd, _The Life of Archbishop Cranmer_ (1831), vol. II, pp. 493–505; Foxe, _Acts and Monuments_, ed. ### Day 5 — George Matheson (1842–1906)

**The jilted-fiancée story is folklore. The honest version is stronger.**

_Matheson's own written account, reproduced in his authorised biography:_

> My hymn was composed in the manse of Innellan on the evening of 6th June 1882. I was at that time alone. It was the day of my sister's marriage, and the rest of the family were staying over night in Glasgow. Something had happened to me, which was known only to myself, and which caused me the most severe mental suffering. The hymn was the fruit of that suffering. It was the quickest bit of work I ever did in my life. I had the impression rather of having it dictated to me by some inward voice than of working it out myself. I am quite sure that the whole work was completed in five minutes, and equally sure that it never received at my hands any retouching or correction. The Hymnal Committee of the Church of Scotland desired the change of one word. I had written originally "I climbed the rainbow in the rain." They objected to the word "climb" and I put "trace."

_On the blindness (Macmillan, ch. I):_

> at the age of eighteen months his mother made the melancholy discovery that his power of vision was impaired. The cause of the defective sight was found to be inflammation at the back of the eyes.

> By the time he entered the Logic Class his eyesight for all practical purposes was gone; he was then in his eighteenth year. It never could be said that he was totally blind; he had his moments of vision.

_On the sisters:_

> two of Dr. Matheson's young sisters labouring earnestly to acquire a knowledge of these languages, so that they might be able to read them to their brother… His college exercises and essays are in her hand-writing.

**CAVEATS TO SHIP — binding:**

- **Do not use the fiancée story. Not even as "some say"** — the framing would lend it credit it has not earned. Macmillan's 1907 authorised biography, written with family cooperation while his sisters were living, contains no engagement, no fiancée, no broken romance anywhere, and says nothing at all about why he never married. It is absent from Matheson's own account, from Julian (1907), and from Carl F. Price (1914, 1923) — the decisive negative test, since romantic tragedy is exactly the genre where such a story would live, and Price attributes the hymn's pain solely to blindness. Even Osbeck, who popularised it, flags it as "never substantiated."
- **Do not substitute a different explanation either.** The common "correction" — that he was grieving the loss of a sister-caretaker to marriage — is also undocumented. **Matheson refused to say what happened, and that refusal IS the angle:** the hymn's authority does not depend on anyone knowing the wound.
- **Do not say he went blind from studying, or that he was totally blind.** Both false; both on Wikipedia.
- **Do not name the sister who married** — Macmillan does not link the 6 June 1882 wedding to any named sister.
- Self-reported: the five minutes and the inward voice are his own testimony, written later.

**Citation:** D. Macmillan, _The Life of George Matheson_ (Hodder & Stoughton, 1907), pp. 181–182 (hymn account), ch. I (blindness), ch. XI (sisters). Hymn first published in _Life and Work_ (Church of Scotland), January 1883.

### Day 2 — the Eden reach

**Augustine, _Confessions_ II.6.14** — Pusey, verbatim:

> Thus doth the soul commit fornication, when she turns from Thee, seeking without Thee, what she findeth not pure and untainted, till she returns to Thee. Thus all pervertedly imitate Thee, who remove far from Thee, and lift themselves up against Thee. But even by thus imitating Thee, they imply Thee to be the Creator of all nature; whence there is no place whither altogether to retire from Thee.

**Augustine, _Confessions_ II.6.13** — Pusey, verbatim (the short form, for a pullquote):

> For so doth pride imitate exaltedness; whereas Thou alone art God exalted over all.

> **DO NOT PRINT "a perverse imitation of God" IN QUOTATION MARKS.** That compact phrasing is **Chadwick's** 1991 Oxford rendering, which could not be fetched and is in copyright. Pusey's wording is "pervertedly imitate Thee." Either quote Pusey or write the idea in the series' own voice, unquoted.

_Why this voice here:_ Augustine's diagnosis of the serpent's offer — sin is not the opposite of God-likeness but a counterfeit of it, which is why it can never actually escape Him.

**Martin Luther, _Lectures on Romans_ (1515–16), scholion on Romans 8:3** — Ficker's edition of the manuscript, verbatim Latin:

> Et hoc consonat Scripture, que hominem describit incurvatum in se adeo, ut non tantum corporalia, sed et spiritualia bona sibi inflectit et se in omnibus querit. Que curvitas est nunc naturalis, naturale vitium et naturale malum.

And, on the same page — **the strongest Day 2 image in the dossier**:

> Natura vero preter se ipsam nullum sibi statuit obiectum, in quod feratur et intendat; se solam videt, querit et in omnibus intendit ceteraque omnia, ipsum quoque Deum in medio, quasi non videat, transit et in se ipsam dirigit. Hoc est 'cor pravum' et 'iniquum'.

— nature sets up no object but itself; it sees, seeks and aims at itself alone, and passes by everything else, God Himself included, standing right in the middle, as though it did not see Him.

Johannes Ficker, _Luthers Vorlesung über den Römerbrief 1515/1516_, Leipzig 1908, pp. 184–85 (= WA 56, 356; = LW 25:345). Ficker preserves Luther's medieval orthography: `Scripture` = _Scripturae_, `que` = _quae_, `querit` = _quaerit_. Source: `https://ia903109.us.archive.org/1/items/luthersvorlesun01fickgoog/luthersvorlesun01fickgoog_djvu.txt`.

> **Two traps here.** (1) The Latin circulating online as Luther's — "_Scriptura enim describit hominem incurvatum in se…_" — is a silently normalized rewrite, NOT the manuscript. Print Ficker's text or none. (2) The published English is LW 25:345, which is in copyright and could not be opened; it rests on secondary citation. If the prose needs English, render it in the series' own words from the Latin above rather than quoting LW. (3) _Incurvatus in se_ as a phrase is Augustinian in root — Luther is the one who makes it the definition of the fallen will. Do not call it his coinage.

**G. K. Chesterton, _Orthodoxy_ (1908), ch. II "The Maniac"** — verbatim:

> The madman is not the man who has lost his reason. The madman is the man who has lost everything except his reason.

> How much larger your life would be if your self could become smaller in it; if you could really look at other men with common curiosity and pleasure…

In the original this sits inside a hypothetical speech Chesterton imagines making _to_ a madman, and is in quotation marks in the text. ### Day 3 — the pivot (over-supplied; cut freely)
