# Newspaper Metaphor — Deep-Dive Research

**Audit date:** 2026-05-16
**Audience:** Founder / pitch-page author for euangelion.app
**Purpose:** Research backbone for pushing the "daily newspaper of the Gospel" metaphor — with a dedicated focus on the founder's most-wanted feature, **a daily Christian comic strip ("THE FUNNIES") in the visual register of newspaper Sunday funnies.**

> "Peanuts but Jesus." — founder, confirmed brief.

This document is structured to be read top-to-bottom by a pitch-page author. Every major topic ends with a **"Use this in the pitch page"** block of three to five concrete, implementable bullets.

## TOPIC 1 — Christian / Theological Newspaper Comic Strips (the spine)

The Gospel has, surprisingly often, been preached in eight inches of newsprint. The history is rich, occasionally controversial, and gives the founder more useful precedent than any other slice of this research. Below is a working dossier of the named precedents, followed by a synthesis section that argues which directions are worth pitching.

### 1.1 B.C. by Johnny Hart — the most direct precedent

Johnny Hart launched *B.C.* on **February 17, 1958**, a daily strip set in prehistoric times featuring a small ensemble of cavemen (Thor, Wiley, Curls, Peter, Clumsy Carp, the un-named "B.C." protagonist) and anthropomorphic animals — including the talking ant-eater. The strip's visual signature is extreme reduction: a horizon line, a single shaded rock, two or three figures rendered with a confident "athlete's-arm" line. Hart "could draw a week's worth of strips in a matter of hours, aided in some measure by the stylized, spare style of B.C." ([Mental Floss, "8 Things You Might Not Know About B.C."](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/547804/facts-about-bc-comic-strip-johnny-hart)).

A recurring device — **Wiley's Dictionary** — has the peg-legged poet Wiley produce a punning "daffynition." Example: *"Rock — to cause something or someone to swing or sway, by hitting them with it."* The dictionary slot is the moment of pure verbal play that lets the strip earn its theological weight elsewhere.

Hart converted in **1984** and taught Sunday school weekly at a Presbyterian church in Ninevah, NY ([Religion News Service, "In B.C. comic strip, artist Hart had a heart for God"](https://religionnews.com/2009/06/19/in-bc-comic-strip-artist-hart-had-a-heart-for-god1/)). From the mid-80s through his death in 2007 the strip became unmistakably Christian on the gospel-shaped holidays — Easter, Christmas, Thanksgiving, Halloween — and increasingly on ordinary Sundays.

**Specific strips that the founder should study:**

- **Easter Sunday, April 15, 2001 ("The Menorah Strip")** — A Jewish menorah burns through seven panels; the candles dwindle in time with Jesus' last words on the cross; in the final panel the menorah's outer arms break away leaving a cross, and a trail of blood leads to an empty tomb with the stone rolled away. The strip drew protest from the Anti-Defamation League and the American Jewish Committee, who read it as supersessionist replacement theology. Hart's defense: *"I noticed one day that the center section of the menorah bore the shape of a cross. I wanted everyone to see the cross in the menorah. It was a revelation to me, one that tied God's chosen people to their spiritual next of kin — the disciples of the risen Christ."* ([Johnny Hart, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnny_Hart); [Breakpoint, "How Johnny Hart Taught Millions About Easter in the Sunday Funny Papers"](https://breakpoint.org/how-johnny-hart-taught-millions-about-easter-in-the-sunday-funny-papers/)).
- **The "To Be Continued" cross** — A wordless Easter strip featuring only the cross with a small *"To Be Continued"* note attached. One panel. Total silence. ([Breakpoint, "How Johnny Hart Pointed to the Cross and Empty Tomb in the Funny Papers"](https://breakpoint.org/how-johnny-hart-pointed-to-the-cross-and-empty-tomb-in-the-funny-papers/)).
- **The Resurrection Cave gag** — A giant stone rolls away from a cave entrance; a caveman walks in to investigate and is invisibly *stepped on* by the rising figure. A pure visual-comedy strip that nevertheless preaches the Resurrection.
- **The "burial site" salesman gag** — Caveman to salesman: *"I'm in the market for a burial site… what have you got in a tomb?"* Salesman: *"I believe we're sold out — let me double-check."*

**Controversy worth knowing about:** A 1996 strip in which a man sings "I Did It My Way" while being crucified prompted real backlash; Hart's later anthology was titled *I Did It His Way* in response ([Goodreads, *I Did It His Way*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/6381882)). The founder should know this name when pitching: it is a precedent for the *"compilation book of the daily strips"* idea, which the newspaper metaphor naturally supports.

**Visual takeaway:** Hart's lesson is that **a stripped-down, low-detail line style absorbs theological weight better than a dense one.** A single rock, a single figure, a single sentence of dialogue is more devotionally serious than a fully rendered scene. Less paint, more prayer.

### 1.2 Peanuts by Charles M. Schulz — the gold standard

Out of roughly 17,800 *Peanuts* strips, [**560 contain a spiritual, biblical, or theological reference**](https://www.beliefnet.com/entertainment/articles/religion-charlie-brown-and-charles-schultz.aspx) — a fact established by Stephen J. Lind in [*A Charlie Brown Religion: Exploring the Spiritual Life and Work of Charles M. Schulz*](https://www.challies.com/book-reviews/a-charlie-brown-religion/) (University Press of Mississippi, 2015). Lind argues Schulz was "a leader in American media when it comes to both the strength and frequency of religious references."

**Linus is the strip's pulpit.** The most-cited example remains [*A Charlie Brown Christmas* (1965)](https://www.movieguide.org/news-articles/charles-schulz-on-faith-in-a-charlie-brown-christmas.html), in which Linus stands center-stage and recites Luke 2:8–14:

> *"And there were in the same country shepherds abiding in the field, keeping watch over their flock by night. And, lo, the angel of the Lord came upon them, and the glory of the Lord shone round about them: and they were sore afraid. And the angel said unto them, Fear not: for, behold, I bring you good tidings of great joy, which shall be to all people. For unto you is born this day in the city of David a Saviour, which is Christ the Lord… And on earth peace, good will toward men. That's what Christmas is all about, Charlie Brown."*

Schulz fought CBS, his producer Lee Mendelson, and animator Bill Melendez to keep the Luke passage in. His justification — *"If we don't do it, who will?"* — has become a touchstone Christian-broadcasting quote. The franchise has run uninterrupted on network television since 1965.

**The complexity to acknowledge:** Schulz was raised Lutheran, taught Methodist Sunday school for a decade, and in his later years described himself as a *"secular humanist"* who still affirmed Christ but no longer attended church. He came to believe other faiths "might also provide legitimate paths to God" ([Freedom From Religion Foundation, Charles Schulz](https://ffrf.org/ftod-cr/item/14922-charles-schulz); [Why Evolution Is True, "Peanuts and theology"](https://whyevolutionistrue.com/2018/08/10/peanuts-and-theology/)). This is useful for the pitch: it is honest to say *"the seriousness of his faith never came at the expense of his seriousness about doubt."*

**The Great Pumpkin** is the strip's most enduring theological device. Linus' annual vigil — sincere, mocked, never rewarded with a visible Pumpkin — has been read by [Boston College](https://bctorch.com/2018/11/01/the-theology-of-the-great-pumpkin/), [The Torch](https://bctorch.com/2018/11/01/the-theology-of-the-great-pumpkin/), and [Reel World Theology](https://www.reelworldtheology.com/its-the-great-pumpkin-charlie-brown-50-years-of-misplaced-sincerity/) as both a tribute to and a critique of religious faith. *"The Great Pumpkin becomes a meditation on the failures of God, a God who never shows up."* This is the line that gives Schulz his enduring credibility: he could write strips that affirm faith and strips that grieve its silences in the same week.

**Visual takeaway:** Schulz invented his line style — perfectly rounded heads, dot eyes, wobbly mouths, triangular bodies — *as a deliberate creative risk*. Comic experts have called the style "those little kids with gigantic heads" and credit it with breaking the previous newspaper-comic convention of more realist child characters ([Screen Rant, "Comic Experts Praise PEANUTS"](https://screenrant.com/comic-experts-praise-peanuts-charles-schulz-creative-risks/); [Toons Mag, "The Artistry of Charles M. Schulz"](https://www.toonsmag.com/the-artistry-of-charles-m-schulz/)). The head is **~1/3 of total character height** — a child cartoonist's proportion, not a real child's — and backgrounds are deliberately abandoned so the emotional weight lives in the face.

### 1.3 Family Circus by Bil Keane — the gentle Catholic example

[*The Family Circus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Family_Circus) — a circular one-panel format about a Catholic family of four kids — is the warmest Christian precedent on this list. Keane was Catholic, sent his kids to Catholic schools, and was honored with the NCEA Elizabeth Ann Seton Award in 1992. His own framing: *"I don't set out to preach at all. I don't consider myself an evangelist, but since I feel religion is a natural part of family life, I show that in cartoons periodically."* ([Beliefnet, "Bil Keane brought quiet faith to the daily comics"](https://www.beliefnet.com/columnists/news/2011/11/family-circus-cartoonist-bil-keane-brought-quiet-faith-to-the-daily-comics-for-50-years)).

Notable devices:
- **Jeffy daydreaming about Jesus in the grocery store** — a panel that became a meme decades before memes.
- **"Not Me" and "Ida Know"** — ghost-like dotted-line children who appear when blame is being deflected. Theological lesson without saying so.
- **The thought-bubble dotted-arc path** — Keane's most famous innovation: a dotted line that traces a child's chaotic path through a yard, house, or town. This is a *narrative device* the founder could literally borrow: trace the path of a wandering soul through a day.

**Visual takeaway:** Keane's circle frame plus a heavier ink line and a clean two-color print is a register entirely distinct from Schulz. It evokes a *bulletin*, not a *broadsheet*. For euangelion this matters because the magazine identity already leans editorial — the daily strip should feel different from the prose around it.

### 1.4 Calvin and Hobbes — the philosophical comparison

Watterson is not a Christian cartoonist in the same sense as Hart or Schulz, but *Calvin and Hobbes* (1985–1995) is the modern American comic strip that most seriously holds the weight of mortality, meaning, and metaphysical doubt. The Christian press has read it generously: [Common Reader at WashU calls it "Bill Watterson's Wisdom Literature"](https://commonreader.wustl.edu/bill-wattersons-wisdom-literature/) — a direct comparison to the Old Testament books of Job, Proverbs, and Ecclesiastes.

**Strips worth knowing:**
- **Calvin's snowmen** — over ten winters (1985-1995) Watterson built a recurring gag of grotesque, philosophical snowmen: snowmen leaping from windowsills, snowmen chopped in half by sleds, snowmen "with a tree staked through its heart" ([CBR, "10 Darkest Calvin and Hobbes Comic Strips"](https://www.cbr.com/calvin-and-hobbes-dark-comic-strips/); [The Blood Shed, "The Demented Snowmen of Calvin and Hobbes"](http://www.theblood-shed.com/the-demented-snowmen-of-calvin-and-hobbes/)). The most-cited is captioned **"The Torment of Existence Weighed Against the Horror of Nonbeing"** — a sentence Camus could have written.
- **The death conversation** — Calvin to Hobbes: *"Mom says death is as natural as birth, and it's all part of the life cycle. She says we don't really understand it, but there are many things we don't understand, and we just have to do the best we can with the knowledge we have."* ([Experimental Theology, "I can't figure out this death stuff"](http://experimentaltheology.blogspot.com/2008/10/theology-of-calvin-and-hobbes-part-3.html)).
- **The final strip, December 31, 1995** — *"It's a magical world, Hobbes, ol' buddy… Let's go exploring!"* The strip ends not with closure but with a permanent open door — which is exactly the affect a daily devotional newspaper wants.

**Why this matters for the pitch:** Watterson proved that a daily strip can sit with hard things without losing its lightness. The visual style — looser line than Schulz, more rendered backgrounds, frequent splash compositions on Sundays — is the high-craft pole the founder should aspire to even if the daily strip lives closer to Schulz.

### 1.5 Modern Christian webcomic precedents

These are the contemporary, web-era artists. They are uneven in craft but worth knowing because each has built an audience for a *daily Christian image-and-caption rhythm* — which is exactly what euangelion is proposing.

**Adam Ford / Adam4d** ([adam4d.com](https://adam4d.com/)) — read by millions; the most theologically dense of the lot. Doctrinal, apologetic, sometimes polemical. Best when it works through a single theological point in 4–8 vertical panels with hand-lettered title cards. Visual style is simple stick-ish characters with hand-drawn lines — closer to *xkcd* than to *Peanuts*. Books: *Implications Abound* and *Thy Kingdom Comics* ([Amazon, *Thy Kingdom Comics*](https://www.amazon.com/Thy-Kingdom-Comics-Curiously-homosexuality/dp/069262953X)).

**Coffee with Jesus / David Wilkie at Radio Free Babylon** ([Coffee with Jesus, InterVarsity Press](https://www.ivpress.com/coffee-with-jesus-ebook)) — single-panel comic built from **old advertising clip art** (the human characters) plus **Sunday-school clip art** (the figure of Jesus). The aesthetic is deliberately retro — 1950s mid-century commercial illustration meets pulpit. Recurring cast: Carl, Lisa, Ann, Kevin, Joe. Tone: irreverent, gently confronting, conservative-evangelical-leaning. This is the only strip on this list whose aesthetic the founder could literally license a clip-art library to replicate.

**Inherit the Mirth / Cuyler Black** ([cuylerblack.com](https://www.cuylerblack.com/inherit-the-mirth-1)) — *"The Far Side meets the Bible."* Single-panel, full-color, watercolor-feeling. Sells in Walmart, Target, Hallmark, Christian retail as cards, calendars, books, t-shirts. Recurring cast is the Bible cast: Adam & Eve, Noah, Moses, David & Goliath, Jonah, Jesus, the disciples. Humor mechanic is **wordplay** that collapses sacred and secular: *Pretzbyterian, Episcopaleozoic, Noah constrictor.* Black is also a pastor — useful for credibility framing.

**Reverend Fun / Dennis "Max" Hengeveld** — ran [1995–2015 on Gospel.com](http://www.gospel.com/ministries/reverend-fun/), totaling **3,230 cartoons** — at one point the most-read Christian cartoon on earth. Style: full color, single-panel, simple gag construction. Subject matter: overzealous ministers, "fresh light on old Bible stories," Christian-subculture gossip and hypocrisy. The longest-running web-native Christian comic — a strong precedent that the founder's daily-strip ambition is sustainable.

**NakedPastor / David Hayward** ([nakedpastor.com](https://nakedpastor.com/)) — the deconstructionist counter-pole. Hayward is a pastor-turned-cartoonist who claims to have been the first to use *"deconstruction"* in the modern faith sense ([Nakedpastor, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakedpastor)). His cartoons speak to *deconstructing* Christians, exvangelicals, LGBTQ+ Christians, survivors of spiritual abuse. Visual style: ink-wash, single-panel, often two characters, often a punchline that lands like a slap. **Mentioned here as the cultural counter-precedent.** Euangelion's strip should *know* this audience exists and decline to belong to either camp — neither apologetic-defensive (Adam4d) nor deconstruction-only (NakedPastor). It should be devotional.

### 1.6 Newspaper-comic-strip aesthetics — what makes a strip *look* like a newspaper strip

This is the visual-language section. Six conventions matter:

1. **Format.** Daily strip = 3 or 4 panels in a horizontal row, roughly 6.5" × 1.75" newsprint footprint. Sunday strip = larger, multi-tier, often 8–12 panels, sometimes with a discardable "topper" first row that papers could cut for space. The founder's strip should live in the **3-panel-daily / Sunday-expanded** rhythm.
2. **Line.** Ink-on-paper line art with confident weight variation. Schulz's line was famously *"the long-practiced grace of an athlete with a golden arm"* ([Toons Mag](https://www.toonsmag.com/the-artistry-of-charles-m-schulz/)). Ketcham (*Dennis the Menace*) used a *"loose, thick cartoony line that seemed to skate across the page"* ([Panels and Prose, "Hank Ketcham and the Art of Dennis"](https://panelsandprose.com/2022/01/02/hank-ketcham-and-the-art-of-dennis/)). The line should look hand-made.
3. **Shading.** **Ben-Day dots** — invented 1879 by Benjamin Henry Day Jr., used in Sunday comics from 1893 onward when New York papers acquired colour rotary web presses ([Wikipedia, "Ben Day process"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Day_process); [Legion of Andy, "Ben-Day Dots: 1890s Sunday Comics"](https://legionofandy.com/2016/03/04/the-history-of-ben-day-dots-part-6-letterpress-printing-1890s-early-sunday-comics/)). Ben-Day uses uniform-sized dots; halftone uses variable-sized dots. **Misregistration** — colour plates shifted out of alignment — *"could even add to the charm of old comics."* This matches the existing euangelion brand spec (riso/halftone duotone with misregistration).
4. **Lettering.** **All caps.** "Text is generally rendered entirely in capital letters, in part because capital letters have no tails… and thus will not overlap letters on the surrounding lines" ([Blambot, "Comic Book Grammar & Tradition"](https://blambot.com/pages/comic-book-grammar-tradition)). Speech balloons (round/oval) for dialogue, cloud bubbles for thought, jagged for shouting, dashed outlines for whispering, rectangular *caption boxes* for narration ([Wikipedia, "Speech balloon"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_balloon)).
5. **Backgrounds.** Mostly absent. Schulz used "frazzled lines" to imply backgrounds when needed but mostly trusted white space. Hart's prehistoric world is a horizon line and a rock. *Less is more.*
6. **Sunday extra: the title-panel logo**, the throwaway-able topper row, the column-width restrictions that historically forced the three-panel grammar.

### 1.7 Synthesis — three directions worth pitching for "Peanuts but Jesus"

Three real options. Each has different costs, different theological reach, different visual debt.

**Direction A — "The Linus Kid" (kid-with-questions, Linus-style).** A single recurring kid character (or two, like Charlie Brown + Linus) sits with another kid, a dog, or a tree, and has Linus-grade theological conversations. The strip is contemporary: no biblical costuming, no first-century setting. Visual debt: Peanuts. Tradeoffs:
- *Pros:* Highest emotional ceiling. Lets you sit with hard questions (suffering, silence of God, doubt) without ever having to depict Jesus. Cheapest to produce because backgrounds and casts can stay tiny.
- *Cons:* Risks being twee. Needs a writer with theological depth and comic timing — rare.

**Direction B — "Biblical Cast, Schulz Pen" (Bible figures rendered as round-headed kids).** Moses, Mary, Peter, Paul rendered in a Schulz/Hart line. Each strip retells or unpacks a Scripture passage. Visual debt: half Peanuts, half *Inherit the Mirth*. Tradeoffs:
- *Pros:* Most natural fit with euangelion's existing *daily lectionary / Bible-365* spine. The strip becomes another way to *read* that day's passage. Highest brand integration.
- *Cons:* Depicting Jesus visually has theological complications (iconoclasm history, ecumenical sensitivities). The strip will draw heat if Jesus' face is anything other than reverent. Hart's menorah strip is a cautionary tale.

**Direction C — "Modern Household, Bible-Shaped Humor" (Family Circus pew).** A contemporary nuclear family (or single adult, or roommates, or church-staff workplace) wrestles with daily life in a way that lets a Scripture verse land each strip. Visual debt: Family Circus + *Coffee with Jesus*. Tradeoffs:
- *Pros:* Most accessible to non-churched readers. Lowest theological risk. Best fit for *Wake-Up* magazine's secular-curious tone.
- *Cons:* Closest to overdone (every Christian publication has tried this). Hardest to make visually distinctive.

**The recommendation:** **Pitch A and C as a pairing.** Direction A is the *spiritual* strip (sits with the wrestle); Direction C is the *cultural* strip (laughs at the mirror). A single character — call him **"Linus"** as a placeholder, or rename him **"Esau"** or **"Job Jr."** — anchors A. A small modern cast anchors C. Together they look like the two-strip slot on a real newspaper funnies page (the serious one + the gag one). Avoid Direction B for v1; reserve it for special holiday strips (Easter, Christmas, Advent) where the audience is primed.

### Use this in the pitch page

- **Lead the section with the historical claim:** *Christians have always quietly preached the Gospel in eight inches of newsprint.* Cite Hart, Schulz, Keane, Watterson by name, and footnote at least Hart's menorah strip and Linus' Luke 2 recitation.
- **Show, don't summarize.** The pitch page should reproduce **one B.C. Easter strip, one Linus Christmas frame, one Family Circus dotted-arc, and one Calvin existential-snowman** as a 2×2 grid above the fold of the funnies pitch. The reader has to *see* the lineage.
- **Name the visual register:** *"three panels, ink line, Ben-Day shading, all-caps hand lettering, no background — Schulz proportions, Hart restraint."* This sentence appears nowhere else; you can own it.
- **Pitch two strips, not one:** the serious Linus-kid strip and the modern-family gag strip, side-by-side on the page like a real funnies section. This is the move that earns the "newspaper" metaphor.
- **Set the theological posture clearly:** *neither apologetic-defensive (Adam4d) nor deconstruction-only (NakedPastor); devotional.* Acknowledging both poles by name reassures sophisticated readers.

## TOPIC 2 — The Newspaper Metaphor on the Web

### 2.1 What digital broadsheets actually do today

**The New York Times.** The current homepage has consolidated half a dozen previous platforms into a single responsive layout that scales across desktop, tablet, and mobile, with editor-controlled story placement. Two custom typefaces, one based on Cheltenham (still the headline face) ([Fast Company, "Why You May Not Even Notice the NYT Major Home Page Redesign"](https://www.fastcompany.com/90129186/why-you-may-not-even-notice-the-new-york-times-major-homepage-redesign); [Sensatype, "Every Font Used by the New York Times"](https://sensatype.com/every-font-used-by-the-new-york-times-in-2025)). Notable for the pitch: NYT still treats the **kicker** (the tiny all-caps section label above the headline) as a sacred unit. *That's the move that says "newspaper" faster than any other.*

**Le Monde.** Even on the digital edition the front page sequence is preserved: opinion column at left, current-affairs photograph, day's editorial in the center, **a cartoon by Plantu at the bottom of the page** ([Le Monde, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Monde)). This is the most direct historical evidence that *a daily editorial cartoon belongs on the front page of a serious paper.* Plantu was Le Monde's daily editorial cartoonist from 1972 to 2021. The slot is part of the paper's identity.

**The Guardian.** The 2025 redesign introduced *section-specific typography and layout variations* so individual sections "have their own visual identity," addressing what one critic called the *"everything looks the same"* problem of news websites ([Creative Boom, "The Guardian's 2025 redesign is the upgrade readers deserve"](https://www.creativeboom.com/news/the-guardians-2025-redesign-is-the-upgrade-its-readers-deserve/); [Norbert Küpper, "The Guardian with a new design in print, web and app"](https://medium.com/@nkuepper/the-guardian-with-a-new-design-in-print-web-and-app-30fab6c97529)). This matters for euangelion because the magazine is multi-rail (devotional / news / funnies / longform / verse-of-the-day): the Guardian model says it's okay — even valuable — for each rail to look different.

**Financial Times.** The FT has built a templated scrollytelling system called the **Story Playbook**, which gives any reporter a way to publish "scrollyblocks" with strong visual depth on a news-cycle timescale ([Source/OpenNews, "How We Improved Visual Storytelling with Templates"](https://source.opennews.org/articles/story-templates-financial-times-reusable/); [npm: @financial-times/n-scrollytelling-image](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@financial-times/n-scrollytelling-image)). The FT also preserves the print-vocabulary "above the fold / below the fold / the basement" in its digital workflow.

### 2.2 The newsletter-style "small papers" — The Marginalian, The Browser, Stratechery

These are the proof points that a single-voice publication can adopt newspaper grammar without being a newspaper.

**The Marginalian** (Maria Popova) — *"undistracted notes on the search for meaning, free, ad-free, AI-free, fully human since 2006"* ([themarginalian.org typography tag](https://www.themarginalian.org/tag/typography/)). Renamed from *Brain Pickings* in October 2021 after fifteen years; included in the Library of Congress permanent digital archive. Visual register: enormous serif body, slow scroll, single-column. Readers describe the experience as *"traversing through an online museum."* This is the closest cousin to euangelion's quiet-dense register.

**Stratechery** (Ben Thompson) — the canonical example of *daily editorial newsletter* design. Stark, plain, narrow column, blue underlined links, no images unless essential. The whole site says *"this is a single voice writing one essay a day, paid to write it."*

**The Browser** — newsletter-as-paper that explicitly curates "five articles a day, with reasons." The brand operates as a deliberately small editor's pick.

**Lesson for the pitch:** these three sites collectively prove that *the newspaper metaphor doesn't require ten reporters and a printing press.* A single voice + a daily cadence + typographic seriousness + a tiny editorial signal (the kicker, the dateline, the byline) is enough to feel like a paper. Euangelion is closer to this than to the NYT.

### 2.3 Awwwards SOTY 2024–2026 — what won, what's relevant

[**Site of the Year 2025 = Igloo Inc.**](https://www.awwwards.com/annual-awards-2025/) (a 3D / WebGL-heavy promotional site — not directly editorial, but worth knowing). [Site of the Year 2024](https://www.awwwards.com/annual-awards-2024/site-of-the-year) included *Don't Board Me, Opal Tadpole, and Lusion v3.* These are product/brand sites, not news. The takeaway is honest: **Awwwards top winners do not skew editorial.** Editorial-feeling wins live in the [Editorial Layout collection](https://www.awwwards.com/inspiration/editorial-layout) and the [Sites of the Day archive](https://www.awwwards.com/websites/2025/) rather than at the SOTY level.

For the pitch, the more useful framing is: *euangelion is intentionally not chasing SOTY-style WebGL. We are building the Marginalian + Plantu + Schulz hybrid.*

### Use this in the pitch page

- **Show four "kickers" stacked vertically:** *DEVOTIONAL · THE FUNNIES · OPINION · VERSE OF THE DAY.* This four-word stack alone communicates "newspaper" without any other proof.
- **Cite Le Monde + Plantu by name.** The serious daily paper has a cartoon on the front page; euangelion will too. This is precedent, not innovation.
- **Cite The Marginalian as the closest cousin** for tone and pace. Adopt one specific tribute — Popova's *"no AI, no ads, fully human"* line — translated to *"fully human, daily, ancient."*
- **Reject the Awwwards-WebGL register on the page itself**. State explicitly that euangelion's metaphor is *typographic*, not *cinematic*. This is a brand position competitors won't claim.
- **Borrow FT's "above the fold / below the fold / the basement" vocabulary** internally and on the page. The "basement" slot at the bottom of every day's edition is the perfect home for THE FUNNIES.

## TOPIC 3 — Specific Interaction Case Studies

For each pattern below: what it is, who does it best (with URL), and one euangelion application.

### 3.1 Scroll-driven animation (scrollytelling)

The genre-defining example is the NYT's **["Snow Fall: The Avalanche at Tunnel Creek"](https://www.nytimes.com/projects/2012/snow-fall/)** (2012, Pulitzer- and Peabody-winning, 3.5M views in week one, 12-minute average dwell time) ([Shorthand, "An introduction to scrollytelling"](https://shorthand.com/the-craft/an-introduction-to-scrollytelling/index.html); [Maglr, "Best scrollytelling examples"](https://www.maglr.com/blog/best-scrollytelling-examples)). The NYT **["Notre Dame Cathedral fire"](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2019/04/16/world/europe/notre-dame-cathedral-fire-spread.html)** explainer is the more recent gold standard.

[**The Pudding**](https://pudding.cool/) is the indie-newsroom equivalent. Recommended specific pieces: [*The Unlikely Odds of Making It Big*](https://pudding.cool/2017/01/making-it-big/) (band trajectories), the [Vogue colorism study](https://pudding.cool/2018/12/pose/), and the [145-years-of-democracy-speech-mentions piece](https://pudding.cool/) — each dwell-time anchor study found ~62% longer engagement on scrollytelling articles vs. flat articles ([CX Fusion, "Scrollytelling"](https://cxfusion.io/en/scrollytelling/)).

**For euangelion:** one premium scrollytelling devotional per major liturgical season (Advent, Lent, Holy Week) — long-form, scroll-triggered, Bible-passage-anchored.

### 3.2 "Roll the stone from the grave" reveal animation

This is the perfectly-fit euangelion-specific application. The interaction pattern: a large dark shape (the stone) sits centered on the page; on scroll or click it slides aside, revealing a brightly lit empty space behind it. Best technical reference is the [View Transitions API on MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/View_Transition_API), reaching Baseline Newly Available status in October 2025 — Chrome 111+, Edge 111+, Firefox 133+, Safari 18+ ([Chrome for Developers, "Smooth transitions with the View Transition API"](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/view-transitions); [Piccalilli, "Some practical examples of view transitions"](https://piccalil.li/blog/some-practical-examples-of-view-transitions-to-elevate-your-ui/)).

**For euangelion:** the Easter Sunday route (`/easter`) opens with a fixed stone on the viewport. Scrolling or tapping rolls it aside; the page rebuilds behind it. *This is the single most justifiable signature animation on the whole site.*

### 3.3 Cinemagraphs on editorial sites

The NYT has used cinemagraphs (single-loop photo+motion) inside features, especially fashion and travel verticals. Most cited modern example: the Notre Dame interactive uses *position-triggered video and animation events* that act like cinemagraphs ([10best Design, "NYT Tries a New Scrolling Technique"](https://www.10bestdesign.com/blog/nyt-tries-a-new-scrolling-technique-that-gets-much-attention/)). For euangelion: a hero candle flame that flickers ~1 frame per 2 seconds; the wind in a wheat field; rain on a window — micro-loops that *say "real, human, breathing"* without screaming.

### 3.4 Magnetic cursor

[Awwwards' editorial on customizing the cursor](https://www.awwwards.com/customize-your-mouse-cursor-inspirational-examples-implementation-tricks.html) is the canonical reference. [Motion+ Cursor](https://motion.dev/magazine/introducing-magnetic-cursors-in-motion-cursor) ships magnetic and zoning features as a library. **For euangelion:** a single quiet magnetic cursor on the daily devotional CTA button — *only there* — so it feels like one moment of deliberate attention, not a gimmick.

### 3.5 Type-on-load headlines / typewriter effect

FT, Bloomberg, and Reuters all use type-on-load for live "breaking news" tickers. The [news-ticker.js typewriter pattern](https://www.jqueryscript.net/animation/rotate-typewriter-effect.html) is the off-the-shelf reference. Pure-CSS implementations exist via `@keyframes` and `animation-play-state` on hover. **For euangelion:** the daily *headline* devotional title types itself on load — once, then settles. Cursor blinks for one beat. This visually says "fresh, printed today."

### 3.6 Bento grids in editorial

(Topic 4 below covers this in depth — used here for completeness.) [Linear.app](https://linear.app), [Vercel.com](https://vercel.com), [Stripe.com](https://stripe.com), [Apple's iPhone product page](https://www.apple.com/iphone), and [Apple at Work](https://www.apple.com/business/) are the canonical product-page bentos. The NYT and FT use **constrained editorial bentos** for section homepages (Climate, Tech), not for the main front page.

### 3.7 Page-turn / view transitions in production

See 3.2. The View Transitions API gives the page-turn pattern essentially for free in modern browsers. The [CSS Script "Realistic Book Page Flip Animation in JavaScript / StPageFlip"](https://www.cssscript.com/book-page-flip-animation/) is the heavier off-the-shelf option. **For euangelion:** the day-to-day navigation in `/bible-365` should use a view-transition crossfade that *briefly* suggests a page turn — not a full skeuomorphic flip.

### 3.8 Breaking-news / off-the-press banner

A horizontal scrolling ticker with **breaking news at 3 a.m.** vibes. Most production implementations are pure CSS keyframes ([Code-Boxx, "2 Ways to Create News Ticker"](https://code-boxx.com/html-css-news-ticker-horizontal-vertical/); [CodePen, "Pure CSS Infinite News Ticker"](https://codepen.io/pnp/pen/rNabEWx)). **For euangelion:** a quiet ticker at the very top of the homepage that cycles *one Bible verse, one liturgical date, one prayer concern.* Three items, slow motion, never aggressive.

### 3.9 Ink-soak / paper-fold transitions

Most credible examples live in motion-graphics templates (Envato Elements, Motion Array). Production-web examples are rare. The CSS-only book-spine effect using `transform-style: preserve-3d` + `perspective` + `rotateY()` is the only common pattern (free-frontend [37 CSS Book Effects](https://freefrontend.com/css-book-effects/)). **For euangelion:** *use sparingly.* One ink-bleed transition for the moment the user first opens the day's devotional. That's it.

### 3.10 Book-spine scroll progress

Replace the standard top-of-page scroll progress bar with a tall vertical "spine" indicator on the left edge with embossed (CSS-shadow) cross-bands like a hardcover Bible. Easy to implement; large brand payoff.

### 3.11 Drop caps in digital editorial

Drop caps date back to 1500s printed books and medieval illuminated manuscripts ([Greenlit Content, "A Complete Guide to CSS Drop Caps"](https://greenlitcontent.com/website/elevate-your-web-typography-a-complete-guide-to-css-drop-caps); [Google Fonts Knowledge, "Drop cap"](https://fonts.google.com/knowledge/glossary/drop_cap)). Standard rule: drop cap = 2–3× surrounding text. CSS `:first-letter` is the cheapest implementation; for ornate caps you can use SVGs. **For euangelion:** already shipped — keep them, but commit to **a tiny gold/cobalt illumination** behind each one to evoke the medieval illuminated manuscript register.

### 3.12 Custom cursors

Use sparingly. See 3.4. For news/editorial sites specifically, a custom cursor *only when hovering over the strip* (it could become a magnifying glass) is a clever editorial micro-interaction with high signal-to-noise ratio.

### Use this in the pitch page

- **Commit to one signature animation: the rolling stone on Easter.** Everything else is supporting. The View Transitions API makes this technically cheap.
- **Pair it with one second-tier signature:** the **type-on-load headline** for the day's devotional title. Together these two moves carry the entire "this is a fresh edition, just off the press" feel without overworking the page.
- **Use a horizontal news ticker at the top** with a strict three-item cycle: today's verse, today's liturgical day, today's prayer concern.
- **Replace the standard top scroll bar with a vertical book-spine progress indicator** on the left edge. Cheap to ship, high brand payoff.
- **Resist scrollytelling everywhere.** Reserve it for one premium piece per liturgical season. Daily devotionals stay typographic.

## TOPIC 4 — Bento Grids for Editorial

### 4.1 The product-bento canon

[Apple's iPhone and Mac product pages](https://www.apple.com/iphone/) are the visual reference. Internal Apple shorthand: *"bento."* Asymmetric tiles, dominant hero tile (~2× the rest), each tile holds one capability ([Jeffrey Hasan, "Apple's Bento Grid Secret"](https://medium.com/@jefyjery10/apples-bento-grid-secret-how-a-lunchbox-layout-sells-premium-tech-7c118ce898aa); [Mockuuups Studio, "Best Bento Grid Design Examples 2026"](https://mockuuups.studio/blog/post/best-bento-grid-design-examples/)). Linear, Vercel, Raycast, Cursor, Stripe, Notion all run versions of this.

**Quantitative claim worth knowing:** "a 2025 analysis of 200 redesigned SaaS homepages found that pages using bento grids in their feature sections increased average time-on-page by 31% compared to traditional stacked sections" ([Landdding, "Bento Grid by Website Category — The 2026 Breakdown"](https://landdding.com/blog/bento-grid-design-by-website-category-where-the-pattern-wins)). Apple-influenced studies cite a 35% dwell-time lift ([Studio Meyer, "Bento Grid Layouts 2026: Why Apple + Google Use Them"](https://studiomeyer.io/en/blog/bento-grid-layouts)).

### 4.2 The critical perspective — bento blurs editorial hierarchy

The most important critical line for the pitch: *"a publication's homepage relies on strict hierarchy by date and prominence — modular layouts blur that signal rather than clarifying it. Long-form editorial sites and blogs rarely benefit from bento treatment, as readers expect linear flow and most attempts to bento-ify article indexes hide the most important content"* ([Landdding](https://landdding.com/blog/bento-grid-design-by-website-category-where-the-pattern-wins)).

In other words: **bento is a product-page pattern, not an editorial-page pattern.** Use it for the *"what is euangelion"* marketing surfaces; reserve **column-and-rail typography** for the daily edition.

### 4.3 Where the NYT and FT actually use bento

NYT and FT use **constrained editorial bentos** for section homepages — Climate Capital at FT, the Tech section page at NYT, the Cooking sub-property — where they have a small number of high-value tiles to surface and the editorial sequence is less critical. They do **not** use bentos for the main front page.

### 4.4 Container queries — the technical enabler

Bento grids only work well when their tiles can restyle themselves based on the container they're in, not the viewport. This is the [CSS Container Queries](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/css-ui-ecommerce-cq) story. Netflix shipped them on Tudum and on e-commerce listings ([web.dev, "Unlocking the power of CSS container queries: lessons from the Netflix team"](https://web.dev/case-studies/netflix-cq)). Chrome's case study explicitly walks through a *news feed* example where the layout adapts based on the available space, not the viewport ([Chrome for Developers, "Container queries case studies"](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/css-ui-ecommerce-cq)).

### Use this in the pitch page

- **Use a bento grid on the marketing / pitch page itself** (the *"what is euangelion"* surface) — it is the right pattern for product showcase.
- **Refuse to use a bento grid on the daily edition.** The daily page is column-and-rail editorial. Stating this constraint on the pitch page is itself a brand move.
- **Cite the 31–35% time-on-page lift** as evidence that the pitch page bento is doing real work.
- **Use container queries to make individual bento tiles reflow** when the user is on a narrow phone vs. a wide tablet — this is the technical detail that says "we know what we're doing."

## TOPIC 5 — Brand-Aesthetic Comic Strip Art (Image-Generation Prompts)

### 5.1 Prompt language that produces real newspaper-strip output

Based on the founder's brand spec (riso/halftone duotone, cobalt + cream + crimson) crossed with what's documented to work in Midjourney and Ideogram for the Schulz/Hart register ([Midlibrary, "Charles Schulz Midjourney style"](https://midlibrary.io/styles/charles-schulz); [OpenArt, "The Best 25 Midjourney Prompts for Comic Style"](https://openart.ai/blog/post/midjourney-prompts-for-comic-style); [ImagineBuddy, "Comic strip style art style prompts"](https://www.imaginebuddy.com/style/create-comic-strip-style-art-with-midjourney-prompt)):

**Master prompt template (Midjourney v6+ / nano-banana / Vertex Imagen):**

> *"3-panel daily newspaper comic strip, [character description], [action], hand-inked black line art with confident weight variation, halftone Ben-Day dot shading, screen-print duotone in cobalt blue and cream off-white paper, single crimson red spot color accent, slight misregistration of ink plates, paper grain texture, newsprint feel circa 1965, all-caps hand lettering in oval speech balloon, rectangular caption box at bottom of final panel, minimal background, single horizon line, Schulz-Hart character proportions (round head 1/3 of body height, dot eyes, triangular body), no photographic detail, no shading gradients — every shadow is dot density, --ar 16:5 --style raw"*

**Pruning notes:**
- Drop *"Schulz"* by name if the model resists named-style prompting. Substitute: *"round-headed kids with dot eyes, wobbly mouth, oversized head proportion."*
- Drop *"newsprint feel circa 1965"* and substitute *"slightly aged newsprint paper texture"* if outputs start coming out yellowed/dingy.
- For **Sunday-format strips:** swap `--ar 16:5` for `--ar 4:5` and add *"8-panel multi-tier Sunday newspaper comic strip with title panel at top."*
- **NEVER** include the word *"Jesus"* in the prompt if generating Direction A (the Linus-kid) — model bias will halo him. For Direction B (biblical-cast strips), prompt *"first-century Galilean teacher in simple robe, brown hair, beard"* and verify each generation by hand.

### 5.2 Eight story-beat seeds for "Peanuts but Jesus"

Each is **(1) concept**, **(2) final-panel caption**, **(3) image-gen visual brief.**

**1. Prayer when angry (Psalm 4 / Eph 4:26).**
- Concept: A small kid is on a porch step at dusk, fists clenched, eyes screwed shut, mid-shout-prayer; the second panel he opens one eye to peek up; in the third he is just sitting, breathing.
- Final caption: *"GOD, I'M MAD. — OKAY. I'M LISTENING."*
- Visual brief: 3-panel newspaper comic strip, small round-headed kid sitting on wooden porch step at dusk, fists clenched in panel 1, one eye peeking open in panel 2, sitting quietly in panel 3, halftone dot shading, cobalt + cream duotone, crimson accent on the kid's sneakers, single horizon line.

**2. Busyness vs. stillness (Ps 46:10).**
- Concept: Kid runs across all three panels carrying a backpack, a laptop, and a calendar; in the final panel he stops mid-stride and a single bird lands on the calendar.
- Final caption: *"BE STILL — AND KNOW."*
- Visual brief: 3-panel strip, round-headed kid in motion blur lines across panels 1 and 2, frozen mid-stride in panel 3, halftone dot shading, single sparrow rendered in crimson, cobalt + cream duotone.

**3. Hope when small (Mt 13:31, mustard seed).**
- Concept: A kid holds a single tiny seed in his palm; in panel 2 he plants it; in panel 3 a giant tree fills the entire frame, dwarfing him.
- Final caption: *"SMALL THINGS HOST BIRDS."*
- Visual brief: 3-panel strip, panel 1 close-up of a small hand with one dot-sized seed, panel 2 wide of a kid digging on a bare hill, panel 3 enormous tree filling the whole panel with a tiny kid silhouette at the bottom, halftone dot shading, cobalt + cream, mustard-seed-yellow crimson accent on a single bird in the tree.

**4. The storm in the boat (Mk 4:35–41).**
- Concept: Three panels of a wooden rowboat in heaving seas; in panels 1 and 2 the disciples are shouting; in panel 3 the boat is calm and someone is asleep in the back.
- Final caption: *"WHO IS THIS, THAT EVEN THE WIND OBEYS?"*
- Visual brief: 3-panel strip, small wooden boat in violent waves, panels 1-2 with rain lines and tilting boat, panel 3 with calm water and a sleeping figure in the stern, halftone duotone cobalt + cream, crimson lightning bolt in panel 1, ink line confident and minimal.

**5. Mary vs. Martha (Lk 10:38–42).**
- Concept: Split-screen single panel — left side is a kitchen with a frantic kid surrounded by pots and dishes; right side is a quiet kid sitting at the feet of a robed figure. Above both panels is a single banner caption.
- Final caption: *"ONE THING IS NEEDFUL."*
- Visual brief: 2-panel comic strip side by side, left panel chaotic kitchen with pots and steam lines, right panel quiet sitting figure at the feet of a robed adult, halftone dot shading, cobalt + cream duotone, single crimson teapot whistling on the left side.

**6. Walking on water (Mt 14:22–33).**
- Concept: Kid steps off the side of a rowboat onto the water and looks down; in panel 2 he is walking and grinning; in panel 3 he is looking at his feet and sinking.
- Final caption: *"AS LONG AS HE LOOKED — HE WALKED."*
- Visual brief: 3-panel strip, panel 1 round-headed kid stepping off a small boat with eyes closed, panel 2 walking on rippling water grinning, panel 3 looking down at feet with water rising past his knees, halftone duotone, single small crimson ripple in panel 3.

**7. The prodigal kid (Lk 15:11–32).**
- Concept: Panel 1 a kid storms out of a house slamming the door; panel 2 he is hungry on a curb in the rain; panel 3 the father runs to meet him before he reaches the door.
- Final caption: *"WHILE HE WAS STILL FAR OFF — HE RAN."*
- Visual brief: 3-panel strip, panel 1 a kid slamming a front door with motion lines, panel 2 the same kid sitting on a curb in the rain with halftone rain streaks, panel 3 a robed father figure running with arms outstretched toward the kid silhouetted in the distance, cobalt + cream + crimson, halftone dots.

**8. The lost coin (Lk 15:8–10).**
- Concept: Single panel: a woman with a candle on the floor of her house, sweeping under a piece of furniture; a single bright crimson coin glints in the dust.
- Final caption: *"ONE COIN. ALL THE LIGHT."*
- Visual brief: 1-panel comic strip, woman with hand-held candle bent down sweeping under a wooden bench, halftone Ben-Day dot shading throughout, cobalt + cream duotone, single crimson coin glowing under the bench, all-caps hand-lettered caption box at bottom.

### 5.3 Bonus seeds to keep in reserve

- **The labyrinth/maze:** kid lost in a corn-maze, the path is clear from above but invisible from inside. *"YOU ARE NEVER AS LOST AS YOU FEEL."*
- **The mustard-seed bird returning:** the bird from Seed #3 comes back the next week in a Sunday strip carrying a seed of its own. *"WHAT'S GIVEN — GIVES."*
- **Lazarus / "Come out":** four panels: closed cave, voice off-panel, stone moves, figure walks out. No caption needed.
- **The five thousand:** single panel of a kid handing his lunch bag to a stranger. Caption: *"FIVE LOAVES. TWO FISH. ONE KID."*
- **The narrow gate:** wide brightly lit gate on the left with a crowd entering; narrow dark gate on the right with one kid pausing. *"BUT THE NARROW ONE LEADS HOME."*

### Use this in the pitch page

- **Reproduce three of the eight story beats** as fully-rendered example strips on the pitch page. Generate them in the founder's confirmed brand palette (cobalt + cream + crimson, halftone, misregistration). This is the most persuasive single move on the page.
- **Print the prompt template** verbatim in a smaller code-block on the page as evidence of repeatable production. This makes the daily-funnies promise *operationally credible*, not just aspirational.
- **Announce the cadence:** *"a new strip every day, six story-beats per week, one larger Sunday format."* This matches real newspaper cadence and gives readers a reason to come back.
- **Reserve one strip per week for a recurring character ("Linus-kid")** and one strip per week for a one-off biblical retelling. The mix matches the *Peanuts + B.C.* historical rhythm.

## SUPPLEMENTAL — Topics the founder should also see

### S.1 The legal / IP question

None of the named precedents (Peanuts, B.C., Family Circus, Calvin and Hobbes) are in the public domain. Aesthetic *inspiration* is legal; visual *copying* is not. The defensible posture for the pitch page is to **name the lineage by precedent** (Schulz, Hart, Keane, Watterson) while producing original characters in an original — but historically-honest — visual register. The riso/halftone duotone brand spec already pushes the work far enough from any single existing strip that the lineage is honored without infringement.

### S.2 The image-generation honesty question

The founder's CLAUDE.md is explicit: *"no arbitrary image use… manifest first, generation last."* This applies to the daily strip too. The recommended posture: generate strips in batches once per liturgical season, hand-curate them, store them as the canonical library, and only generate *new* strips when no library asset fits. Do not generate strips in real-time on user request — the unpredictability of image generation is incompatible with the seriousness of devotional content.

### S.3 The voice question — who writes the captions?

The single most important hiring/role decision attached to the funnies feature is **who writes the caption every day.** Schulz wrote every Peanuts strip himself for 50 years. Hart had a small studio in his later years. The founder needs to decide whether this is **one writer's voice** (highest theological integrity, lowest output) or **a rotating editorial board** (higher output, more variance). For the pitch, the persuasive answer is: *one writer, six days a week, Sundays double-length, captions reviewed by an editor.* That's the newspaper model.

### S.4 The Sunday-edition idea

Real newspapers had two distinct comic formats: the **daily strip** (3-panel, weekday, fast gag) and the **Sunday strip** (full-page splash, multi-tier, lush color). euangelion should adopt the same rhythm: a small daily strip Mon–Sat, a full-width Sunday strip with title panel and 8–12 panels of deeper Scripture engagement. This matches both the **Lord's Day** liturgical instinct and the **newspaper-history** instinct.

### Use this in the pitch page

- **Sign the lineage but own the originality.** A single sentence: *"We owe Schulz, Hart, Keane, and Watterson. We are not them."*
- **Disclose the production model.** *"Generated in batches per liturgical season, hand-curated, written by a single voice, edited weekly."*
- **Promise the Sunday edition.** This is the move that turns a daily strip into a **publication.**
- **Reserve the controversial slot.** State explicitly that Easter, Christmas, and Holy Week will have *high-stakes* strips (the rolled stone, the empty tomb, the cross) and that the rest of the year will be *low-stakes* (the kid on the porch, the prodigal, the mustard seed). Readers respect the spectrum.
