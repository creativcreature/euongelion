# Research: Anatomy of a Mid-20th-Century American Broadsheet

**Audit:** 2026-05-16 — Newspaper Edition specimen page
**Purpose:** Authoritative anatomy reference for the Euangelion newspaper-metaphor specimen page. Every element below is meant to be rendered as a working visual example on the page, with precise newspaper-design terminology used in section dividers and accessibility labels.
**Scope:** American daily broadsheet, roughly 1950–1975 (the late hot-metal and early phototypesetting era), with hooks into the design-school terminology that became canonical in the 1980s–1990s (Harrower, García, Society for News Design).

## 1. Authoritative Sources

Before any HTML is drafted, the page author should be able to defend every choice from one of the following. They are organized from most authoritative (industry standards body, copyright-deposit archive, canonical textbook) downward.

### 1.1 Society for News Design (SND)

[The Society for News Design](https://snd.org/) was founded in 1979 as the Society of Newspaper Designers, renamed the Society of Newspaper Design in September 1981, and became the Society for News Design in October 1997 ([Wikipedia: Society for News Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_News_Design)). Its annual **Best of Newspaper Design Creative Competition** also started in 1979, became the **Best of News Design** in 2009 when magazines were added, and as of the 47th edition in 2026 evaluates roughly 4,200 entries across print, digital, student and non-editorial work ([SND: Annual Creative Competition](https://snd.org/best-of-design-competitions/), [SND47 awards](https://snd.org/snd-2026-awards/)). SND's annual books are the canonical visual reference for award-winning newspaper page design from 1980 onward; the page author should treat SND-honored pages as the reference for any "what good looks like" question.

### 1.2 Poynter Institute

[Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/) publishes the most widely cited working-journalist commentary on layout grammar. The single most useful Poynter piece for this specimen is Mario García's [The Center of Visual Impact](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2002/the-center-of-visual-impact/) (2002), reviewed in section 3.5 below.

### 1.3 Library of Congress: Chronicling America

[Chronicling America](https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/) is the largest open archive of historical American newspapers — over 13 million pages, all in the public domain, digitized through the [National Digital Newspaper Program](https://www.loc.gov/collections/chronicling-america/about-this-collection/) with NEH funding. The program covers newspapers published 1690–1963, so the **late-1950s and early-1960s broadsheet front pages** the Euangelion specimen wants to evoke are directly viewable and downloadable as JPEGs. Use the [Chronicling America search](https://chroniclingamerica.loc.gov/) with a date range of `1955–1963` and a state filter to surface specific named broadsheets as visual references. The [Library of Congress research guide](https://guides.loc.gov/chronicling-america) walks through search and download procedures.

### 1.4 Freedom Forum / Newseum "Today's Front Pages"

The Newseum closed in 2019 but the Freedom Forum continues to maintain a [Today's Front Pages archive](https://www.freedomforum.org/today-front-pages/) showing current-day front pages from hundreds of papers around the world. It is a useful contemporary control set when you want to confirm that a layout convention claimed as "traditional" is still in active use.

### 1.5 The Newspaper Designer's Handbook — Tim Harrower

Tim Harrower's *The Newspaper Designer's Handbook* (McGraw-Hill, seven editions 1991–2013) is the canonical academic reference; Harrower's first book *Contemporary Newspaper Design* (1978) introduced the [Center of Visual Impact](https://garciamedia.com/blog/center_of_visual_impact_where_the_eye_goes_first_on_page_or_screen/) framework that the entire industry adopted ([García Media](https://garciamedia.com/blog/center_of_visual_impact_where_the_eye_goes_first_on_page_or_screen/)). The 7th edition has seven chapters:

1. **Fundamentals**
2. **Story Design**
3. **Page Design**
4. **Photos & Art**
5. **Nuts & Bolts**
6. **Graphics & Sidebars**
7. **Special Effects**

The full 7th-edition page is at [McGraw-Hill Higher Ed](https://www.mheducation.com/highered/product/newspaper-designer-s-handbook-harrower-elman/M9780073512044.html). The 6th edition is downloadable as a complete PDF from [La Salle's ACES archive](http://www1.lasalle.edu/~beatty/310/ACES_CD/books/Newspaperdesigner.pdf). An [Internet Archive borrowable scan of an earlier edition](https://archive.org/details/newspaperdesigne0000harr) is also available. McGraw-Hill hosts a free glossary keyed to the book at [the McGraw-Hill student-resources site](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/0072407611/student_view0/glossary.html) and individual chapter worksheets including [1.12 Writing a Cutline](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/dl/free/0072996692/510538/Harrower6_notes_ch1_12.pdf).

For the cutline-specific worksheet and any other Harrower chapter handouts, the page author should mirror the McGraw-Hill chapter naming conventions verbatim ("Chapter 1: Fundamentals," etc.) when section dividers in the specimen page reference Harrower as the authority.

## 2. The Classic Broadsheet Elements (Precise Terminology)

For each element: the term newspaper designers actually use, what the element does, where it sits on the page, and (where relevant) the historical convention that defines its mid-century form. Every term below is the one the specimen page should use in section dividers and `aria-label`s.

### 2.1 Nameplate / Masthead / Flag (these are three different things)

These three terms are routinely confused, including by working journalists. The American newspaper-design distinction:

- **Nameplate** (American English) is the designed title of the publication as it appears on the front page — the wordmark, in its branded typeface and color, usually spanning the full page width, including any ornamentation, subtitle, motto, or heraldic device. *The Times* of London uses the British Royal Arms between "The" and "Times" inside its nameplate; Scotland's *Daily Record* puts an ornamental lion to the right of "Daily" ([Wikipedia: Nameplate (publishing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nameplate_(publishing))). Location is fixed at the top of page one.
- **Flag** is the most common day-to-day shop term for the same element. "Flag is the name of a newspaper as it's displayed on Page One" ([Ball State](https://blogs.bsu.edu/journalismworkshops/2016/06/07/newspaper-terms-to-know/)). The two terms — nameplate and flag — are effectively synonyms in 20th-century American practice.
- **Masthead** is technically a different thing: the box, usually on the editorial page (page A2 or near the editorials), that lists the names of the publisher, editor, managing editor, owner, frequency, place of publication, subscription rates, and contact information ([Masthead vs Flag](https://thecontentauthority.com/blog/masthead-vs-flag), [Wikipedia: Masthead (American publishing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masthead_(American_publishing))). British English collapses the two — what Americans call the nameplate, the British call the masthead. When journalists adopted the nautical term "masthead" in the early 1800s, it originally meant the name at the top of page one, but by 1900 American practice had moved it to the staff list inside the paper ([Creators Syndicate: Newspaper Nomenclature Has Nautical Nature](https://www.creators.com/read/rob-kyff-word-guy/08/19/newspaper-nomenclature-has-nautical-nature)).

**For the specimen:** use *nameplate* in copy that refers to the page-one wordmark, use *masthead* in copy that refers to the staff/publisher box, and accept *flag* as the friendly synonym for the nameplate.

### 2.2 Ear

The space in the upper-right or upper-left corner of page one, flanking the nameplate, called the **ear**. Historically the ear carries a slogan ("All the News That's Fit to Print"), the date, the weather summary ("Cloudy, high 68"), the edition mark ("Late City Edition"), the price, or a small refer to an inside story ([Ball State](https://blogs.bsu.edu/journalismworkshops/2016/06/07/newspaper-terms-to-know/)). The *New York Times* has used both ears continuously since 1896.

### 2.3 Folio Line

The **folio** (sometimes "folio line" or "running title") is the strip across the top of every interior page that carries the publication's name (often shortened), the section name, the date, the page number, and on the front page the volume and issue number ([Brighthub Multimedia: Newspaper Page Elements](https://www.brighthub.com/multimedia/publishing/articles/81427/), [García Media: What's in a folio line?](https://garciamedia.com/blog/whats_in_a_folio_line/), [Quora: What is folio in a newspaper?](https://www.quora.com/What-is-folio-in-a-newspaper)). The folio is small, set in a sans or thin serif, and exists to orient the reader without competing with editorial content. Folios appear on every page of every newspaper, magazine, and book; their universality is the point.

### 2.4 Dateline

The **dateline** is the prefix at the start of the body text of a story, identifying the city of origin and (historically) the date the story was filed. AP style sets it in all caps followed by the state name and an em-dash with surrounding spaces before the first sentence — `NEW YORK — President Kennedy today announced ...` ([eReleases: AP Style Dateline](https://www.ereleases.com/pr-fuel/how-to-format-a-press-release-dateline-in-different-styles/), [Wikipedia: Dateline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dateline)). For wire stories, the dateline tells the reader which AP, UPI, or Reuters bureau filed the report. Antebellum newspapers used datelines from the first decades of the republic to credit the originating port or city of news brought by ship ([U. Illinois Antebellum glossary](https://guides.library.illinois.edu/antebellum-american-newspapers/glossary)).

### 2.5 Banner Headline / Streamer / Screamer

The **banner headline** (also **streamer** or, when sensational, **screamer**) is the headline that runs across the full width of page one ([Ask Difference: Streamer vs. Banner](https://www.askdifference.com/streamer-vs-banner/), [Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/journalism-jargon-guide/)). Large display headlines did not appear until the late 19th century, when competition between Pulitzer's *World* and Hearst's *Journal* drove the use of attention-grabbing type. Mid-century broadsheets typically used 72-point to 96-point banners for everyday top stories and 120-point or larger for war declarations, presidential deaths, and moon landings.

A **streamer** is functionally identical to a banner; British usage prefers *streamer*, American practice splits between *banner* and *streamer*. A **screamer** carries an editorial connotation of sensationalism (the *New York Post* school) and in UK newsroom slang [Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/journalism-jargon-guide/) the word is interchangeable with *dog's cock* — both meaning an exclamation mark, by extension a headline that screams.

### 2.6 Deck / Drophead / Readout / Subhead

The **deck** is the secondary headline that sits below the main banner, in smaller type, summarizing or expanding the lead. **Drophead** and **readout** are American working synonyms; **subhead** is the catch-all. The U. Illinois antebellum glossary defines decks specifically as the *individual lines* of a multi-line headline, with stacked deck arrangements becoming common during the Mexican-American War era (1846–1848) and distinguished by typographic contrast or horizontal rules ([U. Illinois glossary](https://guides.library.illinois.edu/antebellum-american-newspapers/glossary)). Mid-century practice was a banner + one to three decks, with the deck set roughly 40–50 percent of the banner's point size.

### 2.7 Kicker / Eyebrow / Hammer

- **Kicker** (also **eyebrow**): the small label that sits *above* the main headline, set in smaller, often italic or all-caps type, naming the topic or section ([Merriam-Webster: kicker](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/kicker-definition-meaning), [Skytree Communications: Kickers, Shirttails, and Slugs](https://skytreecommunications.com/kickers_shirttails_slugs.html)).
- **Hammer**: an inversion — the *headline above* is the big bold line and the *headline below* is the smaller explanatory deck. Functionally a kicker turned upside down, used for high-impact storytelling. Sometimes called a **barker**.

These terms are not interchangeable; specimen-page section dividers should preserve the distinction.

### 2.8 Byline

The **byline** is the credit line identifying the author, traditionally `By JAMES RESTON` set in small caps on its own line below the headline ([River: AP Style Bylines and Datelines](https://rivereditor.com/blogs/generate-byline-dateline-journalism)). Bylines were *not* standard practice in the antebellum era — most articles were unsigned or used pseudonyms — and did not become routine until the late 19th century ([U. Illinois antebellum glossary](https://guides.library.illinois.edu/antebellum-american-newspapers/glossary)). The mid-century convention was byline on its own line, sometimes with a title row beneath ("Staff Correspondent of The New York Times").

### 2.9 Lede / Lead Paragraph

The **lede** (deliberately misspelled to avoid confusion with the *lead* metal slug used to space lines of type in hot-metal composition) is the opening paragraph of a news story. The classic AP "inverted pyramid" lede answers who-what-when-where-why in the first sentence. A **NIB** ("news in brief") is a story consisting only of a single-paragraph lede ([Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/journalism-jargon-guide/)).

### 2.10 Jump Line / Continued Line

When a story extends beyond its starting page, the **jump line** is the typographic device that tells the reader where to continue ([Better Homes & Gardens Stylebook: Jump Lines](https://bhgstylebook.com/jumplines/), [WNA: How to improve your story jumps](https://wnanews.com/2018/05/17/newspaper-design-story-jumps/)). Standard form:

- At the end of the column on the starting page: `Continued on Page 4` (or `See WAR, Page A6`), flush right, in bold or italic.
- At the top of the resuming column on the destination page (the **jump page**): `Continued from Page 1`, flush left, with a kicker reading the slug of the story (`WAR`) for navigation.

The whole continued segment is called a **jump**. The destination page is the **jumps page**; mid-century papers often used a designated A-section back page to hold the day's jumps.

### 2.11 Pull Quote / Lift-Out Quote

A **pull quote** (also **lift-out quote** or **call-out**) is a key phrase pulled from the body of a story and set as a display graphic — much larger type, usually 18–36 point, set off by rules above and below or floated in a center column ([Wikipedia: Pull quote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pull_quote), [Creative Pro: How to Attract Attention With Pull Quotes](https://creativepro.com/how-to-attract-attention-pull-quotes/)). Its job is dual: visual relief in a sea of gray, plus an entry teaser that catches a scanning reader. Style sheets typically define exact placement, weight, and rule treatment.

### 2.12 Sidebar

A **sidebar** is a short, related story boxed beside the main story, with its own headline and (sometimes) its own byline. Press Gazette's working definition: "In newspapers, a column beside a page lead" ([Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/journalism-jargon-guide/)). Sidebars carry context (a list of casualties, a timeline, a who's-who), analysis, or a human-interest companion to the main report. Border treatments — boxed, screened (gray-tinted), or just rule-separated — vary by paper.

### 2.13 Cutline / Caption / Cutline Credit

The text under a photograph is, in American newsroom practice, the **cutline**. A *caption* is technically a short title that runs *above* a photo; cutlines are the longer descriptive lines *below* ([Setonhill: Writing a Cutline](https://jerz.setonhill.edu/blog/2014/10/09/writing-a-cutline-three-examples/), [BU DME: Handout on Captions and Cutlines](https://sites.bu.edu/dme/handout-on-captions-and-cutlines/), [Harrower 6th ed. handout](https://highered.mheducation.com/sites/dl/free/0072996692/510538/Harrower6_notes_ch1_12.pdf)). In current practice the two words are used interchangeably; in mid-century practice the distinction was strict. The line giving the photographer's credit — `Photo by Eddie Adams / Associated Press` — is the **cutline credit** or simply the **credit line**, usually set smaller and either flush right at the end of the cutline or directly below the photo.

### 2.14 Cutout / Mortise / Wrap

Three image-layout tricks that all involve breaking the rectangular frame of a photo:

- **Cutout** (also **silhouette**): the background of a photo is removed so the subject's silhouette interrupts the type around it. Used to let a star athlete leap "out" of the picture into the surrounding column.
- **Mortise**: a rectangular notch cut out of one image to inset a second image or block of text inside it. The notched window itself is the mortise.
- **Wrap** (also **runaround** or **text wrap**): the body text reflows around the irregular edge of a cutout or graphic, so the words follow the silhouette.

These tricks were difficult and expensive in hot-metal composition (the type had to be physically cast around the mortise), so they marked a paper as design-conscious; they became routine after the shift to phototypesetting and especially pagination software in the late 1980s.

### 2.15 Drop Cap / Initial Cap

A **drop cap** (or **initial cap**, **initial letter**) is the oversized first letter of a paragraph, dropped down into the surrounding lines so its top aligns with the cap-height of the first line ([Keboto: Drop Cap](https://keboto.org/faq/drop-cap), [Magazine Designing: Drop Caps and Initial Letters](https://www.magazinedesigning.com/drop-caps-and-initial-letters/), [Number Analytics: Mastering Initial Letters and Drop Caps](https://www.numberanalytics.com/blog/ultimate-guide-initial-letters-drop-caps)). Newspaper and editorial conventions:

- A **2-line drop cap** spans the first two lines — the everyday workhorse for inside-page feature openings.
- A **3-line drop cap** spans three lines — the standard for a magazine-style lead or a Sunday section feature.
- A **4-line drop cap** (or larger) is reserved for high-prestige openings: the Sunday Magazine front, a special-section opener, a column flagship.

A **raised initial** (or **stick-up cap**) sits *on* the baseline of the first line but extends *up* above the cap-height. A **versal** is a drop cap with extra decorative treatment (illuminated-manuscript style). Mid-century papers used drop caps sparingly — usually only for the Sunday Magazine and the editorial page — because each one required hand-set type in the hot-metal era.

### 2.16 Index

The front-page **index** (or **inside index**) is the table-of-contents block, usually boxed and rule-separated, telling the reader which page each section starts on: `Sports … D-1`, `Business … C-1`, `Obituaries … B-8`, `Comics … E-4`. Position is conventionally bottom-left of page one, below the fold, or in a column rail down one side.

### 2.17 Refer / Promo Box / Skybox / Teaser

A **refer** ("re-fer" — pronounced like the verb) is a short page-one block promoting an inside story, with a tiny headline, one or two lines of summary, and a page number. A **promo box** is the same idea presented as a more designed unit (usually with a small photo). A **skybox** is a promo box placed *above* the nameplate, on the very top edge of the page ([Brighthub: Newspaper Page Elements](https://www.brighthub.com/multimedia/publishing/articles/81427/), [Gannett's GateHouse Newsroom blog](http://www.gatehouseroom.com/2010/03/22/what-a-nice-newspaper-skybox-looks-like/), [Newspaperdesign.org: Do we need teasers in page one](https://newspaperdesign.org/2018/03/16/discussion-do-we-need-teasers-in-page-one-2/)). A **teaser** is the generic term for any of these. Mid-century broadsheets used refers and promos sparingly; the skybox proliferated in the 1980s after *USA Today* showed that color teasers above the flag drove single-copy sales.

### 2.18 Editorial Cartoon

The **editorial cartoon** (or **political cartoon**) is a single-panel opinion drawing on the editorial page, distinct from the comics page. The mid-century master was Herbert L. Block, signed **Herblock**, at the *Washington Post* — three Pulitzer Prizes (1942, 1954, 1979) plus a shared Pulitzer for Watergate; he coined the term "McCarthyism" in a 1950 cartoon ([Library of Congress: About Herblock](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/herblocks-history/about.html), [Britannica: Herblock](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Herblock), [Herb Block Foundation](https://www.herbblockfoundation.org/herb-block/life-and-work-great-political-cartoonist)). Other defining figures: **Paul Conrad** (*Los Angeles Times*, three Pulitzers), **Bill Mauldin** (St. Louis Post-Dispatch, two Pulitzers, the "Willie and Joe" GI cartoons). The form is single-panel, black-and-white line work, signed and dated, captioned with one or two lines.

### 2.19 Comic Strips / The Funnies / Sunday Color Comics

The **comics page** (or **comics section**) carries syndicated daily strips, multi-panel and printed in the daily issue in black and white. The **Sunday funnies** (or **Sunday color comics**) are the much-larger color comic supplement that ships with the Sunday edition — typically a four-to-eight-page tabloid-format insert.

- Origins: Richard Outcault's **Yellow Kid** is conventionally cited as the first sustained American newspaper comic, debuting in Pulitzer's *New York World* in 1895; Hearst's *New York Journal* poached the strip in 1896 and the first Sunday color comics supplement sold 375,000 copies ([Wikipedia: Sunday comics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_comics), [Wikipedia: The Yellow Kid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Yellow_Kid), [The Nib: The Sunday Funnies' Colorful History](https://thenib.com/color-archive/)).
- By 1906 every major American city had a weekly Sunday comics supplement, distributed by competing syndicates — chief among them **King Features Syndicate**, founded by Hearst, which packaged Krazy Kat, the Katzenjammer Kids, and Betty Boop ([CBS News: The King of Sunday Funnies Turns 100](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/the-king-of-sunday-funnies-turns-100/)).
- Panel-count conventions for the Sunday format: King Features standardized nine panels for the full-page Sunday strip (one used for the title block); United Features and Universal Press standardized two panels for the title ([Wikipedia: Sunday comics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunday_comics)). The dominant Sunday format by the 1960s was the **half-page** (so two strips per page), with the lead strip (Peanuts, Blondie) sometimes still claiming a full page.
- Lettering convention: hand-drawn all-caps inside speech balloons, with **bold** for emphasis and slanted serif italic for thoughts (the thought-balloon convention).

### 2.20 Letters to the Editor

The **letters column** sits on the editorial page (page A6 or A8 in mid-century broadsheets), under a standing kicker — "Letters to the Editor" or "Voice of the People." Format: each letter introduced by its subject as a small bold kicker, the letter's text in normal body type, signed at the end in italic with the writer's name and city (`— John Smith, Brooklyn`). The standing rule that all letters must be signed (no pseudonyms, no initials) is universal in mid-century practice.

### 2.21 Op-Ed

**Op-ed** is an abbreviation of "**op**posite the **ed**itorial page" — not "opinion editorial," a common misreading ([Wikipedia: Op-ed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op-ed), [Etymonline: op-ed](https://www.etymonline.com/word/op-ed), [Britannica: Op-Ed Page](https://www.britannica.com/topic/op-ed-page)). The term goes back at least to 1924 (Oxford English Dictionary); Herbert Bayard Swope's 1921 "Page Op." in the *New York Evening World* is a possible precursor ([Slate: How the NYT op-ed page got started](https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2010/09/a-press-scholar-explains-how-the-new-york-times-op-ed-page-got-started.html)). The modern op-ed page in its now-canonical form debuted at the *New York Times* on September 21, 1970, under editor John B. Oakes. The page is reserved for outside contributors — voices not on the paper's editorial board — and traditionally faces the unsigned house editorials, so the two pages read as a left-right spread of "what we think" and "what you think."

### 2.22 Editor's Note

An **editor's note** is a short bracketed paragraph appended to a story, set in italic, used for clarifications, disclosures (`The author is a paid consultant to ...`), or post-publication updates that don't rise to the level of a correction ([River: Corrections & Editor's Notes templates](https://rivereditor.com/blogs/write-correction-editor-note-template)). Mid-century practice was to set the note in italic, flush left, with `Editor's Note:` in bold roman as its lead-in.

### 2.23 Advice Columns

The **advice column** is the syndicated daily Q-and-A column where readers write in with personal problems and the columnist replies. Two dominant mid-century names, both authored by twin sisters:

- **Ann Landers** — pen name created by Ruth Crowley at the *Chicago Sun-Times* in 1943; taken over by Esther "Eppie" Lederer in 1955; at peak ran in 1,200 daily newspapers with 90 million readers ([Wikipedia: Ask Ann Landers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ask_Ann_Landers), [Jewish Women's Archive](https://jwa.org/thisweek/jul/04/1918/ann-landers-and-abigail-van-buren)).
- **Dear Abby** — founded by Eppie's twin sister Pauline Phillips ("Abigail Van Buren") in 1956, syndicated by McNaught (1956–1966) then Universal Press; over 1,200 papers by 1987 ([Wikipedia: Dear Abby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dear_Abby), [Book Riot: The Rivalry of Dear Abby and Ann Landers](https://bookriot.com/dear-abby-and-ann-landers/)).

Format convention: question opens with `DEAR ABBY:` (or `DEAR ANN LANDERS:`) in small caps, body of the letter in normal type, signed at the end with the writer's pseudonym in italic and small caps (`— PERPLEXED IN POUGHKEEPSIE`). Reply opens with `DEAR PERPLEXED:` and runs in normal type, signed `— ABBY` at the close. Two to four exchanges per day, set in a single-column rail.

### 2.24 Classifieds

The **classified section** (or just **classifieds**) is the back-of-the-book block of small line ads, organized by category, sold by the line. Mid-century categories (universal across American broadsheets):

- **Help Wanted** — split, until the late 1960s/early 1970s, into **Help Wanted — Male** and **Help Wanted — Female**; this was the standard convention before Title VII of the Civil Rights Act and EEOC enforcement closed it down ([GenealogyBank: Help Wanted–Female ads](https://www.genealogybank.com/blog/help-wanted-female-classified-ads-working-women-ancestors.html), [New Republic: Help Wanted—Female](https://newrepublic.com/article/144614/help-wantedfemale)).
- **For Sale** — sub-categories: Furniture, Appliances, Musical Instruments, Sporting Goods, etc.
- **Personals** — initially used by lonely soldiers and pen-pal seekers; proliferated by the late 1960s into the recognizable lonely-hearts form ([Rare Historical Photos: Vintage classified ads](https://rarehistoricalphotos.com/long-lost-personals-vintage-classified-ads/), [Wikipedia: Personal advertisement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_advertisement)).
- **Lost & Found** — the original "missing dog, generous reward" column.
- **Real Estate** — split into **Houses for Sale**, **Apartments for Rent**, **Commercial**, **Land**.
- **Automobiles** — split by make in larger markets.
- **Business Opportunities**, **Legal Notices**, **Public Notices**, **Auctions**.

Typographic convention: 5.5- or 6-point agate type, category headers as small all-caps bold kickers running the width of one or two columns, each ad set as a single justified paragraph with the bold opening phrase serving as a mini-headline (`MAHOGANY DINING SET — Solid, six chairs, leaf, $200 firm. Call WHitehall 6-2391 after 6 PM`).

### 2.25 Obituaries

The **obituary section** (or **obits**) carries the day's death notices, organized either by paid notices first (longer, more detailed, written by families) then by news obituaries (written by staff for prominent deaths). Mid-century broadsheet conventions:

- Format: deceased's name in bold cap-and-lowercase at top, age in parentheses, then a one-paragraph life summary; survivors listed; service details (`Services Tuesday, 10 AM, St. Joseph's Church, Brooklyn`). Set in 8- or 9-point body type, single column.
- Standing kicker labels: **DEATHS**, **DEATH NOTICES**, **OBITUARIES**.
- A separate **In Memoriam** section runs paid anniversary notices — `In loving memory of John Doe, who passed away ten years ago today. Always remembered, never forgotten. — His wife and children`. Format is short, often poetic, deeply standardized.

### 2.26 Weather Panel

The **weather panel** (or **weather box**) carries the day's forecast in a small boxed unit, traditionally placed in the ear of page one and expanded on an inside page. Components:

- Today's high/low and conditions (`High 72 / Low 54, Partly Cloudy`).
- Yesterday's high/low and the year-to-date precipitation total.
- A small icon (sun, cloud, rain, snow) representing today's conditions.
- An **extended outlook** (next two or three days) below.
- A small **weather map** showing the national front map and major-city forecasts.

Before USA Today rewrote the conventions in 1982, the weather map was a small black-and-white inside-page item — monochrome contouring with frontal lines, high/low pressure symbols (H and L), and city names ([García Media: USA Today turns 30 — Part 3](https://garciamedia.com/blog/usa_today_turns_30-part_3-a_weather_map_that_created_a_global_tsunami/), [AccuWeather: The demise of the surface weather frontal map](https://www.accuweather.com/en/weather-blogs/weathermatrix/the-demise-of-the-surface-weather-frontal-map/2060)). USA Today's full-page color weather map (designer George Rorick) was copied globally within five years.

### 2.27 Stock Ticker / Market Summary

The **market summary** runs at the top of the business section, listing the day's closing values for the major indices (Dow Jones Industrial Average, S&P 500, NASDAQ in later years), the change in points, and the percentage change with up-arrows (▲) for gainers and down-arrows (▼) for losers. The convention is bold green or "+" for up and red or "−" for down (color was rare in mid-century print; black bold and `+/−` did the work).

The **stock tables** beneath were the agate-type grid of every listed stock, organized alphabetically by ticker, with columns for 52-week high/low, dividend, P/E ratio, volume in hundreds, day's high/low/close, and net change. Set in 5.5-point agate, five or six stocks per column-inch, ten to fifteen columns across a full broadsheet page. Reading a 1965 stock table was a learned skill; the columns are abbreviated to the point of cryptography ([Boston University Library: Newspapers and Periodicals](https://library.bu.edu/invest/newspapers-and-periodicals), [The Nest: How to Read Stock Prices From a Newspaper](https://budgeting.thenest.com/read-stock-prices-newspaper-29096.html)).

### 2.28 Horoscope

The **horoscope** column runs the day's astrological forecast for each of the **twelve zodiac signs** (also called the **twelve houses** of the zodiac in pop-astrology vocabulary, though strictly the houses are a separate concept from the signs). Each sign gets one or two lines of forecast. Two syndicated giants of the mid-century:

- **Sydney Omarr** (Los Angeles Times Syndicate) — daily Sun Sign Horoscope in 200+ newspapers, with 50 million copies of his annual books sold ([Wikipedia: Sydney Omarr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sydney_Omarr), [Washington Post obituary](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/local/2003/01/04/astrologer-columnist-sydney-omarr-76-dies/21322a0b-f091-416e-b8a2-de564edbfa53/)).
- **Jeane Dixon** (Universal Press Syndicate) — daily column in nearly 400 newspapers worldwide, the most famous American astrologer of the post-war era ([Wikipedia: Jeane Dixon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeane_Dixon)).

Format: sign name in small caps with dates (`ARIES (March 21–April 19)`), one or two sentences of forecast, in column-rail single-column form.

### 2.29 Crossword

The **crossword puzzle** as the American newspaper standardized it was the work of Margaret Petherbridge Farrar, the first crossword editor at the *New York Times* (1942–1968) ([Wikipedia: Margaret Farrar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Farrar), [Wikipedia: The New York Times crossword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times_crossword), [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/crossword-became-american-pastime-180973558/), [TIME: How the Toughest Moments Shaped the Crossword Puzzle's History](https://time.com/5811396/crossword-history/)). Farrar's rules — still the standard:

- **180-degree rotational symmetry** in the black-square pattern (the grid rotated half-turn is identical to itself).
- **Minimum word length of three letters.**
- **No unchecked squares** — every letter belongs to both an across and a down word.
- **Grid sizes**: 15×15 for daily puzzles (standardized 1950); 21×21 for the Sunday puzzle (Farrar's compromise from her initial 23×23 in 1942).
- The *Times* first crossword ran Sunday, February 15, 1942; daily crosswords began September 11, 1950.

Clue numbering: every cell that starts a new word (down or across) gets a small numeral in its top-left; clues are listed `ACROSS` and `DOWN` in separate columns, each clue numbered to match its starting cell, with the clue text followed by the answer length in parentheses or just left blank (`1 ACROSS  Capital of France (5)`).

### 2.30 Word Search

The **word search** (or **word find**) is the simpler letter-grid puzzle: a rectangular grid of letters with a list of hidden words to circle. Far less prestigious than the crossword; usually grouped with the puzzles page or the kids' page. Mid-century syndicated word-search columns: King Features's "Wonderword."

### 2.31 Sports Page

The **sports section** (called **Section D** or **Section S** depending on the paper) carries scores, standings, game stories, and columns. Standard mid-century elements:

- **Scoreboard / Roundup** — a boxed summary at the top of the page listing the day's scores for all major leagues, in two- or three-line summaries (`Yankees 5, Red Sox 3 — Mantle HR`).
- **Standings** — league standings tables, each league as a sub-box: team name, W, L, percentage, games behind (`GB`).
- **Box scores** — the abbreviated stats grid for each major game, by player. Conventions: visiting team on top line, home team on bottom; AB/R/H/RBI for batters; IP/H/R/ER/BB/K for pitchers. By the late 1960s defensive stats were dropped from the lineup to save space, retaining only the error list in the game summary ([Wikipedia: Box score (baseball)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box_score_(baseball))).
- **Lead column** — the marquee sports columnist (Red Smith at the *New York Herald Tribune* / *New York Times*; Jim Murray at the *Los Angeles Times*; Shirley Povich at the *Washington Post*), set with a head-shot mug and a standing column kicker.
- **Game stories** — the lead game story usually got a full-width banner and a five- or six-column layout.

### 2.32 TV / Radio / Event Listings

The **TV listings** (called **TV Log** at many papers) ran the day's broadcast schedule organized first by airtime then by channel, in a text-based "log" format modeled on the program logs broadcasters themselves maintained ([Wikipedia: TV listings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_listings)). Print TV listings were a standard daily feature from the late 1950s to the mid-2000s. Sunday editions carried an expanded **TV magazine** or **TV book** supplement with the whole week's prime-time grid. Format: time in bold flush left, channel number(s) in a smaller cluster, program title, short description.

**Radio listings** ran in a smaller daily box, organized by station call letters with the day's notable programs flagged.

**Event listings** (concerts, theater, lectures, club meetings) ran in a daily "What's Going On" or "Calendar" column, set in small justified paragraphs grouped by category and day.

### 2.33 Display Advertisement

A **display ad** is any in-paper ad larger than a single classified line — usually with illustration, headline type, and white space, sold by the column-inch. Mid-century display ads (department-store ads, automobile ads, movie ads) used **woodcut illustrations**, **line drawings**, or **clip art** rather than photography, because line art reproduced cleanly on newsprint where halftones often went muddy ([Britannica: Graphic design 1945-75](https://www.britannica.com/art/graphic-design/Graphic-design-1945-75), [Fast Company: Clip art changed the way we view ourselves](https://www.fastcompany.com/90855809/clip-art-changed-the-way-we-view-ourselves-and-the-world)). Photography began replacing illustration in display ads in the late 1950s, but illustration held on at department stores (the Lord & Taylor / Bonwit Teller line-drawing tradition) into the 1980s.

### 2.34 Infographic

The **infographic** (information graphic) in its modern form is a 1982 invention of *USA Today*, where designer George Rorick's full-color weather map and Sam Ward's daily front-page chart ("USA Today Snapshots") launched the genre ([Wikipedia: USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today), [García Media: USA Today's weather map global tsunami](https://garciamedia.com/blog/usa_today_turns_30-part_3-a_weather_map_that_created_a_global_tsunami/)). Before 1982, broadsheets used **line charts**, **bar charts**, and **tables** built from rule-and-type composition — Wall Street Journal's stipple-portrait illustrations are the parallel pre-USA Today tradition. Mid-century election-night results came as plain tables; weather maps were monochrome contour-and-front diagrams. The Euangelion specimen should distinguish *pre-1982* infographics (line, table, monochrome map) from *post-1982* (colored, iconic, summary).

### 2.35 Almanac / On This Day

The **almanac column** (sometimes **On This Day** or **Today in History** or **Day in History**) runs a small standing box with anniversaries, notable births and deaths, the day's saint's name (in Catholic-leaning papers), and the year of the paper's founding. AP's "Today in History" was syndicated widely; the *New York Times* "On This Day" ran on the editorial page.

### 2.36 Travel Column

The **travel section** (Sunday only at most papers) carried a weekly destination feature, a Q-and-A advice column on travel logistics, calendars of fares and rail/air schedules, and short news items from the wire services' travel desks. The standing daily **travel column** was rarer; many papers ran one only in the Sunday section.

### 2.37 Errata / Corrections Box

The **corrections column** (or **errata**) is the standing daily box, usually on page 2, that lists corrections to errors in previous issues ([Wikipedia: Correction (newspaper)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correction_(newspaper))). Standard form: `An article on Page 1 yesterday incorrectly identified the senator as [X]. He is [Y]. The Times regrets the error.` The convention is that corrections appear with the same prominence the original error received and on a fixed page so readers know where to find them.

### 2.38 Section Dividers

Inside a multi-section broadsheet, each new section gets a fresh **section front** with its own **section flag** (a smaller version of the nameplate carrying the section name — **Sports**, **Business**, **Style**, **Arts**) plus its own folio convention (`B-1`, `C-1`, `D-1`, etc.). The section flag uses the same wordmark family as the nameplate, sized down. The first inside page of each section often gets a **section dek** — a one-line standfirst summarizing what's in the section that day.

### 2.39 Subscription / Circulation Panel

The **subscription box** carries the rates and instructions for home delivery: daily, Sunday-only, daily-and-Sunday rates; mail rates; address and phone for the circulation desk. Usually appears in the masthead box on the editorial page, or in a small standing box on the back of the A-section.

### 2.40 Footer / Colophon

The **colophon** (or **publisher's imprint**) is the small block, usually at the very bottom of the editorial page or on the back page, naming the printer, the publisher, the copyright, and the address (`Copyright © 1965 The New York Times Company. Published daily by The New York Times Company, 229 West 43rd Street, New York 36, N.Y. Second-class postage paid at New York, N.Y.`). Required by USPS second-class mailing rules.

## 3. Layout Grammar

### 3.1 Rule of Thirds

The **rule of thirds** divides the page into a 3×3 grid; the four intersection points are the visual focal points. Applied to a broadsheet front page roughly 15 inches wide by 22.75 inches long, the rule places the *Center of Visual Impact* (the dominant photo) at one of the upper intersections and lets the nameplate, banner headline, and refer/promo blocks flow around it ([Fiveable: Newspaper Layout Fundamentals](https://fiveable.me/editorial-design/unit-8/newspaper-layout-fundamentals/study-guide/ObFtrRuiCVJvMFt8)). The broadsheet's roughly 1:1.5 aspect approaches the **golden rectangle** (1:1.618), and the rule of thirds approximates the golden ratio's focal points well enough for everyday design work.

### 3.2 Above the Fold / Below the Fold / Basement

A broadsheet ships folded in half horizontally for newsstand display. Everything visible on the upper half — typically the top 11 inches — is **above the fold**; everything on the lower half is **below the fold**.

- **Above the fold** is the prestige zone: nameplate, banner, dominant photo, lead story start. Single-copy purchase decisions are driven by what shows above the fold on a folded paper on a rack.
- **Below the fold** carries the second- and third-tier stories. The mid-century convention was that the *second* most important story of the day went bottom-left below the fold.
- **The basement** is the strip across the bottom of the page (above or below the fold) used to "hold the page up" — a long horizontal story or anchor piece that visually balances the top ([Press Gazette](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/journalism-jargon-guide/), [Neil Benson: The lost language of newspapers](https://www.neilbensonmedia.co.uk/home/writing-journalism/the-lost-language-of-newspapers)). Also called the **anchor**. The basement is to the bottom of the page what the skybox is to the top.

### 3.3 8-Column vs 6-Column Grid

American broadsheets historically used a **9-column** or **8-column** grid in the hot-metal era (1900–1970), with the wider grid driven by the demand for more ad inches per page. The shift to a **6-column** grid began in the late 1960s and was effectively universal by 1981, driven by both reader-comfort research (longer column measures are easier to read) and a desire for cleaner pages. By the mid-1980s most American broadsheets had standardized at 6 columns of roughly 1.83 inches (11 picas) wide, with 1- to 2-pica gutters ([Wikipedia: Column inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_inch), [Fiveable: Newspaper Grid Structures](https://fiveable.me/advanced-editorial-design/unit-8/newspaper-grid-structures/study-guide/6Rc2XECLdNLLHdmx)).

The Euangelion specimen, evoking a mid-1960s broadsheet, should default to **8 columns** for the news pages and **6 columns** for the feature/Sunday-magazine spreads, with a 1-pica gutter between.

### 3.4 Gutters and Column Rules

- **Gutter**: the vertical white space between columns. American broadsheet standard is 1 to 2 picas (about 1/8 to 1/4 inch).
- **Column rule**: a thin vertical line (a "rule") set between columns, used most often when gutters are narrow or when story boundaries need extra visual separation. The U. Illinois antebellum glossary defines *rule* as a metal strip set typographically to produce a horizontal or vertical line, used as a border between columns, around advertisements, or separating articles and headline decks ([U. Illinois glossary](https://guides.library.illinois.edu/antebellum-american-newspapers/glossary)). Mid-century American broadsheets used column rules selectively — *Times*, no; *Tribune*, yes.

### 3.5 Center of Visual Impact (CVI)

The **Center of Visual Impact** is the single dominant element on a page — almost always the dominant photo or illustration — that the eye lands on first. Mario García's standing rule: the CVI should be **three to five times larger in mass** than anything else on the page, and on a front page it should sit **as high as possible** so it survives the fold for newsstand display ([Poynter: The Center of Visual Impact](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2002/the-center-of-visual-impact/), [García Media: Center of Visual Impact](https://garciamedia.com/blog/center_of_visual_impact_where_the_eye_goes_first_on_page_or_screen/)). Pages with no CVI — where all the photos are sized similarly — read as visually weak and unhierarchical. Harrower introduced the concept in *Contemporary Newspaper Design* (1978) and it has been the foundation of American newspaper page-design pedagogy ever since.

### 3.6 Art-Side vs Text-Side Balance

A traditional broadsheet front page is mentally divided into an **art side** (where the dominant photo and any other images live) and a **text side** (where the headline-heavy stories sit). The convention is to put the CVI/art on one half and balance with type on the other, so the page reads as a visual conversation rather than two competing photos. Skilled designers cross this rule deliberately for impact; the rule exists so the violation registers.

### 3.7 Jump Pages

The **jumps page** is the designated interior page (often the A-section back page) where the day's continued stories land. Best practice ([WNA: How to improve your story jumps](https://wnanews.com/2018/05/17/newspaper-design-story-jumps/)): give the jumps page enough room for the visual elements that didn't fit on the front, and put at least one photo or graphic on the jumps page so a reader arriving from a jump line doesn't bounce off a wall of gray.

### 3.8 Hierarchy of Headlines (Traditional Point Sizes)

A mid-century American broadsheet's headline hierarchy, from top down:

| Element | Typical size (points) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Banner / streamer** | 72–96, up to 120+ for historic news | Full width of page one |
| **Spread headline** (multi-column, single-story top) | 48–72 | 4- to 6-column |
| **Second lead headline** | 36–48 | 2- to 4-column |
| **Inside-page top headline** | 30–42 | 2- to 4-column |
| **Deck / drophead / subhead** | ~40–50% of banner | One to three decks |
| **Kicker / eyebrow** | 14–18, usually italic or all-caps | Above the headline |
| **Standfirst** | 14–18 italic | Below headline, above lede |
| **Byline** | 10 small caps | Bold credit line |
| **Body copy** | 9 to 10 on 10 to 11 leading | Legibility Group type |
| **Cutline** | 8 to 9 italic or bold | Below photo |
| **Folio** | 7 to 8 | Top of each page |
| **Agate** (classifieds, box scores) | 5.5 to 6 | The tiniest standard size |

## 4. Typographic Conventions

### 4.1 Headline Typefaces by Paper

- **New York Times** — **Cheltenham**, adopted 1906 and still the defining headline face. Bertram Goodhue designed Cheltenham in 1896; Morris Fuller Benton expanded it at American Type Founders. In the early 2000s the *Times* commissioned Matthew Carter to draw a custom proprietary version (**NYT Cheltenham**) that remains the masthead face today ([Madegood Designs: Cheltenham Font](https://madegooddesigns.com/cheltenham-font/), [Sensatype: Every Font Used by The New York Times](https://sensatype.com/every-font-used-by-the-new-york-times-in-2025)).
- **Washington Post** — **Bodoni** (the Benton-redrawn-for-ATF 1911 version) for headlines, in title case, with a proprietary derivative **Postoni** for current production ([Type Network: Washington Post Evolving typography](https://typenetwork.com/articles/washington-post), [Washington Post archive: A New Face Type, 1998](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/1998/02/11/a-new-face-type/0f0b93fe-42c0-4667-bfeb-d32229fabf82/)).
- **Wall Street Journal** — historically Latin Bold Condensed (the Hoefler-Frere-Jones redesign added Retina later).
- **Chicago Tribune** — historically a Bodoni-derivative slab-and-serif blend.
- **Los Angeles Times** — historically Bookman-derivative, later proprietary.
- **USA Today (1982 onward)** — Gulliver and a proprietary sans for headlines, deliberately breaking the Cheltenham/Bodoni serif consensus.

Cheltenham and Bodoni together account for the majority of American newspaper headline practice in the 1950s–1970s. Both are serif display faces with strong contrast and ball terminals on a few key letters; both are recognizable at 96 points across a newsstand.

### 4.2 Body Typefaces — The Legibility Group

The **Legibility Group** is the family of serif body faces designed by Chauncey H. Griffith at Mergenthaler Linotype starting in 1925, intended specifically for newspaper Linotype work — low stroke contrast, wide open counters, ball terminals, designed to survive the heavy ink and poor paper of high-speed letterpress newsprint ([Wikipedia: Legibility Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legibility_Group), [Linotype.com: Corona LT](https://www.linotype.com/292/corona-lt-family.html), [Mergenthaler Linotype 1935: The Legibility of Type](https://archive.org/details/Mergenthaler1935TheLegibilityOfType)). The series:

- **Ionic No. 5** (1925) — the first and most successful; the workhorse of American newspaper body type from the late 1920s through the 1950s.
- **Textype** — similar but with a lower x-height for more delicate structure.
- **Excelsior** — reduced x-height, intended for rubber-roller presses; common in European wide-column papers.
- **Opticon** — heavier, to compensate for under-inked printing favoring halftones.
- **Paragon** — lighter, to compensate for over-inked printing favoring text and headlines.
- **Corona** (1941, Griffith) — condensed, large on the body; designed for narrow newspaper columns; adopted widely in the 1940s and 1950s by American dailies. By the late 1950s Corona was the body face of the *New York Times*, the *Boston Globe*, and dozens of mid-market dailies ([Fonts In Use: Corona](https://fontsinuse.com/typefaces/3896/corona)).

The *NYT*'s current proprietary body face is **NYT Imperial**, a redrawn descendant of Edwin W. Shaar's Imperial (1957) — another Legibility-Group successor designed explicitly to compete with Corona ([Sensatype: NYT fonts](https://sensatype.com/every-font-used-by-the-new-york-times-in-2025)).

### 4.3 All-Caps Usage

All-caps usage in mid-century broadsheets is reserved for:

- **Datelines** (`NEW YORK, March 14 —`).
- **Kickers and eyebrows** (`SUPREME COURT`).
- **Standing column kickers** (`DEAR ABBY`, `LETTERS TO THE EDITOR`, `EDITORIAL`).
- **Stock ticker symbols** (`IBM`, `GM`, `XOM`).
- **Headlines on tabloids only** — broadsheets keep their main headlines in cap-and-lowercase, reserving all-caps banners for the *New York Post* school.

The convention is so strong that an all-caps banner on a respectable broadsheet reads as a special-edition signal (war declared, president shot).

### 4.4 Drop Cap Conventions

Mid-century broadsheets used drop caps sparingly because hot-metal composition made them labor-intensive. Standard practice:

- **Editorial page** — opening editorial gets a 3-line drop cap, set in the headline face (Cheltenham, Bodoni).
- **Sunday Magazine front feature** — opens with a 4-line drop cap, often illuminated with a small piece of decorative art.
- **Op-ed lead column** — 2- or 3-line drop cap.
- **Style/Arts section opener** — 3-line drop cap.
- **News stories** — no drop cap. The lede sits flush left in body type.

### 4.5 Ligatures, Old-Style Figures, Small Caps

Mid-century Linotype body faces include the standard `fi`, `fl`, `ffi`, `ffl` ligatures by default — they were physically part of the matrix. Old-style figures (1234567890 sitting at body height rather than cap height) were used in feature copy and credit lines but rarely in news body (where lining figures match the all-caps datelines). Small caps were standard for bylines and continuation slugs (`See WAR, A6`) — the small caps appeared in the Linotype matrix as a distinct font.

For the Euangelion specimen page (a digital simulation rather than hot metal), the right choice is to enable OpenType ligatures, old-style figures for feature/Sunday content, lining figures for news and stock-table content, and small caps for bylines and `Continued on` slugs.

## 5. Devotional Translation Hints

Short notes on how each classic broadsheet element could plausibly carry devotional content for the Euangelion newspaper-edition specimen. These are scaffolding suggestions, not finished copy.

- **Obituaries → "Saints Remembered" or "In Memoriam"** — the day's commemorations from the liturgical calendar plus user-submitted prayer remembrances of departed family. Format: full name, dates, one-paragraph life summary, intercession line. The standing kicker should read **SAINTS REMEMBERED** or **IN MEMORIAM** to preserve the funeral-page typographic gravity.
- **Stock ticker → Scripture "ticker"** — verses and reference codes with **▲** marking a verse trending in user reflections that day and **▼** marking one quieting. The ticker symbol slot becomes a four-letter book abbreviation (`PSAL`, `ECCL`); the price slot becomes the verse reference (`23:1`). Reads as familiar financial-table grid, decodes as spiritual.
- **Horoscope → "Twelve Houses" daily Scripture** — repurpose the twelve-sign convention into twelve devotional houses (one per liturgical hour, or one per virtue, or one per Beatitude), with a one-line daily passage for each. Preserves the column-rail Omarr/Dixon format exactly; substitutes Scripture for star-chart.
- **Sports → "This Week's Saints"** — turn the scoreboard into a liturgical calendar: the week's feast days as a roundup box, with the day's principal saint as the "lead game." Box scores become saint vitae (year of birth/death, region, principal works). The marquee sports column becomes a weekly devotional column from a recurring writer.
- **Weather → "Spiritual Climate"** — preserves the boxed weather-panel typography exactly. Today's "high/low" becomes today's consolation/desolation; the icon becomes a liturgical color (red for martyrs, white for feasts, green for ordinary time); the extended outlook becomes the week's lectionary outlook.
- **Classifieds → "Calls"** — Help Wanted becomes ministry openings (`MINISTRY WANTED — Adult catechist needed for parish RCIA, Brooklyn, contact ...`); Personals becomes prayer requests (`PRAYERS WANTED — For my mother, hospice care, third week ...`); Lost & Found becomes intercession requests for the lost; Real Estate becomes pilgrimage and retreat-center listings.
- **Advice column → "Dear Editor"** — replaces *Dear Abby* with a daily Q-and-A in which a reader writes in with a spiritual question and a panel of writers replies. Preserve the `DEAR EDITOR:` / `DEAR PERPLEXED:` / `— EDITOR` format verbatim.
- **Travel → "Pilgrimage" column** — biblical-geography column running a weekly destination feature: one week the Sea of Galilee, the next Iona, the next Mount Athos. Sunday-only standing column matches the historical travel-section cadence.
- **TV listings → Liturgical service schedule** — the log-format grid (time first, channel second, program third) translates directly: time first, parish/church/community second, service name third (`6:00 AM — St. Joseph's, Mass`, `6:30 AM — Online, Morning Prayer`). Mid-century TV-log typography (bold time, agate body) maps perfectly onto a service schedule.
- **Editorial cartoon → "Daily Parable"** — single-panel illustration on the editorial page, drawn weekly by a contributing artist, captioned with one or two lines from Scripture or the Fathers. Preserves the Herblock single-panel slot exactly.
- **Crossword → "Lectio Crossword"** — Farrar rules preserved (180° rotational symmetry, no unchecked squares, 15×15 daily / 21×21 Sunday), with clues drawn from Scripture, hymns, saints, liturgical seasons, and church history.
- **Horoscope's twelve-sign rail → "Calendar Houses"** — alternative reading: twelve liturgical seasons or twelve traditional Christian virtues, each with its sign-equivalent date range and daily forecast/reflection.
- **Almanac / On This Day → "Calendar of the Communion"** — the day in church history: anniversaries, feast days, year-of-the-founding for the issue's masthead.
- **Stock tables full grid → "Reflection Index"** — agate-type grid of the day's most-shared passages with reflection counts. Preserves the unreadable-dense visual signature of the financial page; rewards close attention with content the casual scroller misses.

## 6. Single-Page Anatomy Checklist

Tick off each as the specimen page is built. The label on the left is what the section divider, `aria-label`, or caption should say verbatim. Source-of-truth column points back to the section above.

### Page-One Anatomy (above the fold)

- [ ] **Nameplate (Flag)** — designed wordmark, spanning full page width. §2.1
