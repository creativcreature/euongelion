Today’s Lead · Devotional · Ecclesiastes 3 · Day 134 of the Year

Be still. He has not gone anywhere.

A field guide for the morning the urge is to run — and the right answer is to sit down at the same table you woke up at and wait.

By The Editors · Photograph by Staff

New JerusalemThe phrase that opens the third chapter of Ecclesiastes — "To everything there is a season, and a time to every purpose under heaven" — is read most often as a poem about acceptance. Read it as a poem about refusal and the chapter becomes harder, and more useful. The Preacher is not consoling the reader. He is telling the reader that whatever is being lived through is one season among many, and that the soul's first instinct — to fix it, end it, sprint past it — is the very instinct that lengthens it.

She was not waiting for anything in particular. That is the part the camera could not catch and the part the Preacher in Ecclesiastes spent twelve chapters circling: the discipline of sitting in the same place when the room is not promising anything back. Her husband, Frank, taught civics at the high school across the road for thirty-two years and died on the morning of his retirement party in 2014. The pew was already old then. The pew is still where she goes.

The trouble with stillness in 2026 is that the marketplace has noticed it. Stillness now sells. There are eleven different applications on the average phone whose icons promise it, each with a notification system that interrupts the stillness in order to remind you to acquire more of it. The Preacher would have found this funny in the same way that the Preacher found everything funny, which is to say funny in the way a person laughs at a thing because the alternative is to break.

The honest report is that most readers of this newspaper will not become Mrs. Renfrew. The pew is not the move. The discipline she has — the small unmoved center — is portable, and that portability is the gift of the Christian morning practice we are trying, every day, to lay out on this front page. A chapter. Four verses sat with. A cup of coffee that gets cold because it was placed on the table to be steam, not to be drunk.

What the third chapter of Ecclesiastes actually contains, after the inventory of seasons, is a turn the reader can miss if they have heard the famous list too many times. The Preacher writes: "He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart, so that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end." The world is set in the heart. The Hebrew is ʿolām — a word that means both "long duration" and, in later usage, "eternity." Whatever has been set in there is too large for one season to hold. That is the source of the restlessness. That is also the source of the rest.

The trouble with stillness is that the marketplace has noticed it. The discipline the Preacher names is not for sale. — from today’s lead

Tomorrow's reading turns the corner into chapter four, which is darker and noisier. Today, the assignment is small and unfashionable. Sit in the same place for ten minutes. Read the same eight verses twice. Do not try to make them yield anything. The thing they are made out of — the ʿolām set in the heart — is not yielded; it is uncovered, and slowly, and never on schedule. The work is not to find the answer. The work is to be in the room when the answer arrives, in a form you may not recognize, and possibly after the coffee is cold.

Mrs. Renfrew, after the photograph was made, opened the Bible on her lap to the page she had been reading and read for another twenty minutes. We did not interrupt her. The light moved. The pew was the same.

Sidebar

Five ways to keep the hour

If sitting still for ten minutes feels theatrical, the following five disciplines have been recommended in our reader mail over the last six months. None of them is a hack.

  • Read the same passage twice. The second time is the prayer. The first time was the introduction.
  • Stay at the table after the cup is empty. Two minutes. The leaving is the noise; the staying is the practice.
  • Put the phone in the next room. Not face-down. Not on silent. In the next room. This is a small physical fact and it is the thing that works.
  • Name one thing you are afraid of. Out loud, once, to the empty kitchen. The naming is what loosens the grip.
  • End with a single sentence. Yours. Not borrowed. Even if it is, "I do not know what to ask for."

From the Editor’s Desk

Why we changed the paper.

By The Editor

Yesterday's edition introduced a redesign that some of you noticed at once and some of you, judging from the mail, have not noticed at all. Both responses are correct. The redesign is meant to be the kind of change that, on a second cup of coffee, registers as "oh, it was always like this" — which is the highest compliment a redesign can earn.

The two changes worth naming: the front page is now organized like a real broadsheet, with a banner headline, a single lead devotional, and a daily comic strip in the basement; and the Soul Audit, formerly a button, is now a tile called Letters to the Editor. The tool is the same. The framing acknowledges that what you write to us is, in fact, a letter — and that we owe you a reply, in the long run, in the form of the devotional plan we build for you.

Letters are read by a human within twenty-four hours. The replies are written by a human, edited by another human, and only then assisted by the same composition tools that help us assemble each morning's edition. The order of operations matters.

Op-Ed · Opposite the Editorial

Against the velocity of certainty.

A note on what the modern Christian conversation has lost by getting faster.

By A Guest Writer · contributing editor

The Christian internet, by which I mean the small loud archipelago of accounts and substacks and podcasts where most American believers under fifty now form their first opinions on most theological questions, has a settled rhythm. A claim is made, in twenty-eight seconds. Three responses are made, in forty seconds. By the next morning the claim has been adjudicated, a side has won, and an unrelated claim has taken its place. The cycle is not new. The speed is new, and the speed is doing something to the soul that no editorial has yet adequately named.

What the speed costs is not, primarily, accuracy. The internet is full of people fact-checking each other; that work, broadly, is done. What the speed costs is the kind of patience that doctrine requires to land. Augustine writes, in the seventh book of the Confessions, that he was "still being formed" by the questions he could not yet answer. He did not mean he was waiting to find the answer in a podcast. He meant that the question was the formation. Skipping it — claiming the answer too soon — removed the very pressure that the question was meant to apply.

The modern Christian conversation rewards the skip. The thirty-second answer wins the day. The slow believer, the one who is still being formed, loses the timeline and so loses the room.

What is asked of us, then — and the editor will forgive a guest writer for saying it bluntly — is to refuse the speed in our own practice even as we participate in the room. To read the question twice before answering. To allow that a believer is sometimes more theologically serious for not having an opinion yet than another believer is for having one. To return to the four-hundred-year-old hymn that already knew the answer that this morning's substack is in a hurry to invent.

This newspaper is one attempt at that refusal. It publishes once a day. It edits before it ships. It does not "take." It sits.

And it asks the reader to sit with it, in the not-quite-time-yet pace of a season that has not yet asked the next question.

Dear Editor · A Standing Daily Column

"I can’t pray when I’m angry."

Two letters from readers, two replies from this desk. Names withheld; cities included by request.

By The Editor

Dear Editor · Tulsa

I can't pray when I'm angry. I sit down to do my morning reading and the prayer just stops in my throat. I read the verse, I close the book, and I am still angry. Last week was a bad week and most of my mornings looked like this. I am not interested in being told to "calm down first." That doesn't work. What do I do?

— A reader in Tulsa

Reply

The premise of your question is the answer: "I can't pray when I'm angry." Half the Psalms — David at the cave, the Sons of Korah at the gate, the unnamed Psalmist who in Psalm 137 prays a sentence so violent the lectionary still cannot read it on Sunday morning — are the work of believers who could not pray any other way. Anger is prayer with the volume up.

Pray the anger. Pray it out loud, in the kitchen, to no one but the empty room. The words do not have to be respectable. They have to be true. The respect comes later, after the anger has been seen.

If even the angry prayer will not come, the assignment for the morning is one verse, read once, and a single sentence written in the margin of your notebook: "I am angry. I am still here." That is enough. Tomorrow may be the same. The morning after that, the prayer that arrives may not be the one you were trying for, and may be the one that was needed.

Dear Editor · Brooklyn

How do I tell my mother I no longer believe what she raised me to believe — without breaking her heart? She is 78. She thinks her job is unfinished if I don't believe what she believes. She is also dying.

— A reader in Brooklyn

Reply

You don't. Not on the timeline you are thinking of. The conversation you are describing — "Mom, I no longer believe what you taught me" — is a conversation about you. The conversation she needs from you, with the months she has left, is a conversation about her. About her own mother. About the day she met your father. About the prayer she has said every morning since 1972. Ask her what she would have done differently. Ask her what she is afraid of now. Listen.

It is not dishonest to leave the theological accounting for later. The accounting belongs to her God, not to you, and she has been on better terms with that God for longer than you have been alive. Trust the relationship she has with the One she taught you about. Then sit with her. Hold her hand. Pray with her when she asks, even if the prayer is not yours; the saying of it is the kindness.

After she dies — and not before — the work of telling the truth about your own belief will still be there. It will take the rest of your life. There is no rush.

Travel · Saturday Column

"Postcard from Capernaum."

A small Galilean fishing town that holds two-thirds of the public ministry of Jesus.

By The Editor · filed from the north shore

Sketch · Sea of Galilee · north shore

There is almost nothing left of it. Stand on the basalt foundations of the old synagogue — the one mentioned in Luke 7, where the centurion's servant was healed at a distance — and you can see the entire town in a single glance. A few stone walls about waist-high. The cleared outline of Peter's house. A modern church suspended on stilts above what archaeologists believe was the room where the paralyzed man was lowered through the roof. The lake is two hundred yards away. The fishing boats still come in at the same hour.

The conventional pilgrimage stops here, takes a photograph, reads the relevant passage from a guide, and moves on to the Mount of Beatitudes. The longer visit, recommended for the morning, is to walk down to the water at sunrise. There is a small public beach — the beach where the resurrection breakfast in John 21 is traditionally placed, charcoal fire and all. You sit on the basalt. The light comes up over the Golan to the east. The boats put out.

What hits you is the size. Capernaum is small. The radius of all this — the synagogue, Peter's house, the tax booth where Matthew was sitting when he was called — is about eight hundred feet. Three quarters of the public miracles in the Gospels happened inside a space you could walk across in four minutes. The kingdom did not need scale. The kingdom needed a small town with a lake and a few people who would come when they were called.

The bus back to Tiberias leaves at nine. You will not need the whole morning, and you will also need the whole morning. Take the morning.

Saint Remembered

Helen Wycoff Mathers

1933 — 2026 · Lincoln, Nebraska

Helen, of Lincoln, died at home on Wednesday morning, eight days short of her ninety-third birthday. She is survived by three children, eleven grandchildren, and a sister in Topeka with whom she had not spoken in forty-seven years over a disagreement none of the family any longer remembers.

She taught Sunday school at First Presbyterian for fifty-one years. Her last class, in 2020, met by telephone, one child at a time, because she did not trust the video screens. The class learned the Beatitudes by heart that year. They had not learned them by heart in any prior year.

She kept the same pew. She left the same tip. She forgave one specific person who never knew they had been forgiven. Memorial service at First Presbyterian, Lincoln, Saturday 23 May at 11 a.m. In lieu of flowers, the family asks for any small kindness, named or not, performed on her behalf.

Saint Remembered

The Rev. Dr. Marcus B. Owens

1942 — 2026 · Boston, Massachusetts

Marcus, of Boston and previously of Selma, died on Tuesday morning after a brief illness, at home, with his wife of fifty-eight years reading the Twenty-third Psalm aloud in the room. He was eighty-three.

An AME pastor for forty-six years, a chaplain at MIT for the final twelve, and a marcher across the Edmund Pettus Bridge in 1965 at the age of twenty-two. He is most remembered for the gentleness of his preaching — never raised his voice in the pulpit, on principle, "because the gospel does not need to be shouted." He was wrong about exactly one thing, by his own count: the date of the Resurrection in a 1987 Easter sermon, off by a calendar year due to a typo in his notes. He corrected it the following Sunday, at length, with laughter.

Funeral at Charles Street AME, Boston, Friday 22 May at 10 a.m. The choir will sing "Wade in the Water." His pew is the one on the left side near the door.

Continued from page A1 · STILL

…The pew was the same.

We left Mrs. Renfrew on the front page at 5:40 a.m., reading. By 7:00 she had moved to the parish kitchen and made coffee in the same percolator she has used since the second Eisenhower administration. By 7:30 she was at her own kitchen table, alone, the radio off. Her daughter calls every Saturday at nine. The hour between is the hour she keeps.

This is the smaller, less photogenic part of the practice that Ecclesiastes 3 actually names. The big moments — the season, the time, the purpose under heaven — are described in chapter 3 in the abstract. The thing that gets you through them, the small hour of unmoved kitchen, is in the rest of the book: scattered across chapter 2's gardens, chapter 4's two-are-better-than-one, chapter 9's eat-thy-bread-with-joy. The pew is the dramatic part. The kitchen is the practice.

The reader of this paper is not expected to keep Mrs. Renfrew's hours. The reader is expected to find the equivalent. A bench at the train station. A specific chair in a specific room. The car, in the parking lot of the building, before the meeting begins. The unmoved center is portable. It has to be discovered, not installed. It is the work of the morning, and the morning after that, and the morning after that.
