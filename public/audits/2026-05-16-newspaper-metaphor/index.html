01

Brief.

Euangelion already wears the broadsheet aesthetic. What it's missing is the front-page feeling: density, surprise, a small daily ritual that says "today's edition is its own thing."

Two anchors confirmed: add THE FUNNIES (tone: "Peanuts but Jesus") and adopt a bento layout (a broadsheet front page is structurally a bento — lead headline dominates; opinion is narrow; comics tile is small). The founder also asked for interaction polish — cinemagraphs, scroll reveals, Awwwards-tier feel — without slipping into gimmick.

How to use this page

Every pitch card has a Yes / Maybe / No control and a notes field. Your selections save to localStorage automatically — you can close the tab and come back. The REVIEW MY PICKS button at the bottom-right shows everything you've marked and lets you copy it all to your clipboard to paste back to me.

Every interaction idea is live-demoed in place. Hover, scroll, click — see what it actually does. Nothing on the live homepage is touched; this page is its own sandbox.

01.5 · Research

What we studied before pitching anything.

Five topics, ~7,500 words of deep-dive, ~40 sources. The full document lives at research-deep-dive.md. Below: the load-bearing findings the rest of this page assumes.

Finding 1 — Christian newspaper comic strips already exist. We're not inventing.

The lineage in numbers

  • Schulz · Peanuts — 560 of ~17,800 strips with religious or biblical content. Linus' Luke 2 reading on 1965 CBS still airs annually. Established by Stephen J. Lind in A Charlie Brown Religion (UP Mississippi, 2015).
  • Hart · B.C. — daily strip from 1958–present; openly Christian from 1984. The Easter 2001 menorah-to-cross strip drew ADL/AJC protest; Hart's later anthology I Did It His Way is the canonical book.
  • Keane · Family Circus — Catholic, NCEA Elizabeth Ann Seton Award 1992. The dotted-arc path of a wandering child is his most famous innovation.
  • Watterson · Calvin & Hobbes — not Christian per se, but read as "Wisdom Literature". The deranged-snowmen captioned "The Torment of Existence Weighed Against the Horror of Nonbeing" is the canonical existential-strip example.
  • Modern Christian webcomics — Adam4d (apologetic, doctrinal), Coffee with Jesus by David Wilkie (clip-art mechanic, irreverent), Inherit the Mirth by Cuyler Black ("The Far Side meets the Bible," single-panel, full-color), Reverend Fun (1995–2015, 3,230 cartoons). Plus NakedPastor as the deconstructionist counter-pole.

Finding 2 — Daily editorial cartoons belong on the front page of a serious paper.

Le Monde + Plantu, 1972–2021

Le Monde's front page sequence — even on the digital edition — preserves: opinion column at left, current-affairs photograph, day's editorial in the center, a cartoon by Plantu at the bottom of the page. Plantu was Le Monde's daily editorial cartoonist for 49 years. The slot is part of the paper's identity. This is the most direct historical evidence that a daily strip belongs at the front, not buried.

Use the funnies slot at the bottom of the homepage — visible without scrolling on desktop, just below the fold on phone.

The Guardian (2025 redesign)

Section-specific typography per rail

The 2025 redesign gave each section "its own visual identity" — addressing the "everything looks the same" problem of news websites.

Permission for our funnies tile, Bible-365 rail, and devotional reader to look genuinely different from each other.

FT Story Playbook

Templated scrollytelling system

The FT preserves "above the fold / below the fold / the basement" vocabulary in its digital workflow. The "basement" is the bottom-of-page slot.

Adopt the basement metaphor. Funnies live in the basement of every day's edition.

The Marginalian

"Undistracted notes on the search for meaning, free, ad-free, AI-free, fully human since 2006"

Maria Popova's site — enormous serif body, slow scroll, single-column. Readers describe it as "traversing through an online museum." Closest cousin to euangelion's quiet-dense register.

Borrow the tone-disclaimer move. Our version: "fully human, daily, ancient."

Awwwards SOTY 2024-26

Igloo Inc., Don't Board Me, Opal Tadpole

Honest read: top Awwwards winners do not skew editorial. They skew WebGL/3D promo sites. Editorial-feeling wins live in the Editorial Layout collection, not at SOTY.

Reject the Awwwards-WebGL register. Euangelion's metaphor is typographic, not cinematic. State this as a brand position.

Finding 4 — Bento is a product-page pattern, not an editorial-page pattern.

The critical line that decides where to use bento on our site

"A publication's homepage relies on strict hierarchy by date and prominence — modular layouts blur that signal rather than clarifying it. Long-form editorial sites and blogs rarely benefit from bento treatment, as readers expect linear flow…"

— Landdding · "Bento Grid by Website Category"

Implication: use bento on the marketing homepage (the "what is euangelion" surface). Use column-and-rail typography on the daily edition (the actual devotional reader). Stating this constraint on the pitch page is itself a brand move.

Quantitative evidence for the marketing-page bento: Landdding's 2025 analysis of 200 redesigned SaaS homepages found 31% increased time-on-page for bento feature sections vs. traditional stacked. Apple-influenced studies cite 35% lift. Bento earns its keep on marketing surfaces.

Finding 5 — Commit to one signature animation, support it with one second-tier.

The animation budget

The research argues against animation everywhere. The pattern that wins is: one signature animation + one second-tier signature + everything else quiet.

  • Signature: the rolling stone on Easter. View Transitions API reached Baseline Newly Available in October 2025 (Chrome 111+, Edge 111+, Firefox 133+, Safari 18+). The technical foundation is essentially free. The route /easter opens with the stone fixed on the viewport; scroll/tap rolls it aside; the page rebuilds behind it.
  • Second-tier: type-on-load headline. FT, Bloomberg, and Reuters all use this for live "breaking news." For us: the daily devotional title types itself on load — once, then settles. Cursor blinks for one beat. Visually says "fresh, printed today."
  • Quiet ticker at the very top with three items (today's verse, today's liturgical day, today's prayer concern). Slow motion. Never aggressive.
  • Book-spine progress on the left edge instead of the standard top scroll bar. Cheap. High brand payoff.
  • Drop caps with tiny gold/cobalt illumination behind each one. Medieval-manuscript register. Already half-shipped.

The constraint to declare on the pitch page

The research is unambiguous on one point: generated comic-strip art lives in batches, hand-curated, stored as a library asset. No real-time AI on user request. No daily-strip generation pipeline. This matches the CLAUDE.md rule ("manifest first, generation last") and protects the devotional seriousness of the content. Strips are generated once per liturgical season, ~30 at a time, hand-reviewed, and shipped.

Full source list (priority-ordered) in the research deep-dive. Top three: Lind's A Charlie Brown Religion, Hart's I Did It His Way, the Charles M. Schulz Museum's Biblical Job page.

02 · The Funnies

"Peanuts but Jesus" — built, not described.

Christians have always quietly preached the Gospel in eight inches of newsprint. Hart, Schulz, Keane, Watterson — four canonical strips with theological reach. Below: the lineage in their own visual register, three style directions for a Euangelion daily strip, and eight sample strips drawn fresh in the recommended direction.

The lineage — four cartoonists who already did this.

Before pitching what our strip should be, here is what already exists. (Detailed deep-dive: research-deep-dive.md.)

Schulz · Peanuts

560 of 17,800 strips with religious content

Linus' Luke 2 recitation in A Charlie Brown Christmas (1965) is the most-watched explicit Gospel reading on American TV. Schulz fought CBS to keep it. "If we don't do it, who will?"

Take the visual proportions — round head ≈ ⅓ body height, dot eyes, wobbly mouth, no nose. The face carries everything.

Johnny Hart · B.C.

The Easter Menorah strip (Apr 15 2001)

Hart converted in 1984; ran openly Christian Easter/Christmas strips for two decades. Visual restraint: a horizon line, a single rock, two figures, no backgrounds. Less paint, more prayer.

Take the radical reduction — strip everything that isn't the emotional beat. Hart could draw a week's strips in hours because the form is so spare.

Bil Keane · Family Circus

Catholic, NCEA Seton Award, 1992

"I don't set out to preach… since I feel religion is a natural part of family life, I show that in cartoons periodically." Innovations: the dotted-line path of a child wandering through a house; the "Not Me" ghost.

Take the circular single-panel format as a deliberate counterweight to broadsheet rectangles. Distinct from the prose around it.

Bill Watterson · Calvin & Hobbes

"Bill Watterson's Wisdom Literature"

Not a Christian cartoonist per se, but the modern strip that most seriously sits with mortality, meaning, doubt. The deranged-snowmen series is captioned "The Torment of Existence Weighed Against the Horror of Nonbeing."

Take the permission to hold hard things lightly. The final strip's line — "Let's go exploring" — is the affect a daily devotional newspaper wants.

The visual register, one sentence

Three to four panels, ink line, Ben-Day halftone shading, all-caps hand lettering, no background — Schulz proportions, Hart restraint.

Ben-Day dots are the printing technique invented in 1879 by Benjamin Henry Day Jr.; used in Sunday comics from 1893 onward when New York papers acquired color rotary web presses. Misregistration of ink plates "could even add to the charm of old comics" — which dovetails with the existing euangelion riso/halftone brand spec. Wikipedia · Ben Day process.

Three style directions — pick the visual lane.

The research argues for pairing two strips on one funnies page, like a real newspaper: a serious daily (Direction A) + a gag daily (Direction C), with Direction B reserved for liturgical-holiday specials. Below each direction is a freshly-generated real strip in that style — these are not the existing devotional-print paintings; these are bespoke, drawn-for-this-pitch comic-strip art.

Direction A · The Linus KidMy pick

A single recurring child — Schulz proportions, working-class hair, plain striped shirt — sits with the wrestle. No biblical costuming. No first-century setting. Theology lives in the caption, not the costume. Highest emotional ceiling, lowest production cost.

Cast One recurring kid (placeholder name: Linus) + a stuffed lamb Cadence 4-panel daily, expanded Sunday Risk Writer dependency — needs theological depth + comic timing Visual debt Peanuts
Pick this direction

Direction B · Wild-Hair & the LambAlt

A small boy + a stuffed lamb that comes alive in his imagination — the lamb representing Jesus, calm and observing. Watterson register: more expressive line, dynamic poses, atmospheric backgrounds. Higher craft ceiling, harder to sustain daily.

Cast Wild-haired boy + Lamb (alive only to him) Cadence 4-panel daily, splash Sunday Risk Lamb-as-Jesus is theologically suggestive — needs careful framing Visual debt Calvin & Hobbes
Pick this direction

Direction C · Family CircusAlt

Circular one-panel format. A modern household, gentle ink-wash shading, a dad reading the Bible aloud while three kids drift in and out. Most accessible to non-churched readers, lowest theological risk. Reads as bulletin not broadsheet — which is its strength: it doesn't compete with the prose around it.

Cast One household (parent + 2-3 kids) Cadence 1-panel daily, expanded Sunday Risk Closest to overdone — every Christian publication has tried this Visual debt Family Circus + Coffee with Jesus
Pick this direction

Direction D · The Two on a RockAlt

Two bald figures on a flat rock, single horizon, no setting. B.C. by Johnny Hart register: extreme reduction, daffynition wordplay, theological one-liners. Easiest to produce, hardest to make emotionally serious. Better as a side strip than the main one.

Cast Two recurring figures (Wiley & Peter as placeholders) Cadence 3-panel daily Risk Reads as one-joke if the writer isn't sharp Visual debt B.C. (1958–2007)
Pick this direction

My recommendation

Pair Direction A and Direction C on one funnies page, like a real newspaper has two strips side by side. The Linus Kid carries the serious wrestle. The Family carries the gentle daily-life note. Reserve Wild-Hair & the Lamb for Easter/Christmas Sunday strips where the audience is primed for higher craft. Skip Two on a Rock for v1.

Why pair two? Because a real funnies page has two strips. One can be quiet; the other can be wry. Readers who don't connect with one might land on the other. Schulz himself ran 1 strip; Hart and Keane shared one slot on most papers — Euangelion can run two from day one.

Strip 01

The Boat in the Storm

Mark 4 · 35–41

Charlie-Brown confessional voice. The lamb (silent stand-in for Christ) is calm; the protagonist isn't. The final caption — "I keep waiting until the boat goes under" — is the most quotable line in the set. Sets the voice for the whole strip.

Strip 02

Mary kept the seat.

Luke 10 · 38–42

Introduces a second character — Martha — for dialogue interplay. The kitchen + impossible dish-stack gag is Schulz-tier visual comedy. The final caption — "Martha kept the schedule. Mary kept the seat" — is the most epigrammatic line of the set; it reads aloud the way Lind says Schulz's best one-liners did.

Decision

Strip 03

Hope — one string left.

G.F. Watts 1886 · re-imagined

The Linus-est of the set. Hope as the lyre with one string remaining, eyes blindfolded, still tuning. The single small star in panel 2 carries the entire theology without preaching it. Reserve this for a slow week.

Decision

Strip 04

The same circle three times.

Chartres labyrinth · contemplative tradition

A pure-visual gag: she walks the labyrinth, reaches the center, walks back out, and is right where she started. The caption — "some days I walk the same circle three times and call it a journey" — names the spiritual experience without naming the theology. Halftone pavement is the best ink-work in the set.

Decision

Strip 05

She did not faint.

Luke 1 · 26–38

The only strip in the set where the protagonist speaks — a single word, "OKAY." That restraint is the entire joke. The final caption acknowledges the reader's own response: "I would have fainted." Hope-with-doubt, voiced.

Decision

Strip 06

Grace before the meal.

after Chardin · c. 1740

The quietest of the eight. An empty bowl. Grace anyway. The final panel's halftone window-glow is the one moment of "warmth" in the set. Lands on the fourth read, the way the best Schulz strips do.

Decision

Strip 07

Jesus never mentioned how long to wait.

Matthew 13 · 31–32

The most overtly named strip — "Jesus" appears in the caption. Charlie-Brown gag delivered straight: kid plants the seed, then stares at the dirt for a day waiting. The reader laughs first, then realizes that's their actual spiritual posture.

Decision

Strip 08

Half the psalms are angry.

Psalm 4 · Ephesians 4:26

The most theologically permissive strip — the reader is told it's okay to be mad at God. This is the line that distinguishes Euangelion's voice from the apologetic-defensive register (Adam4d) without falling into deconstruction-only (NakedPastor). Devotional, not polemical.

Decision

What's reserved for a Sunday edition

Real newspapers had two distinct comic formats: the daily strip (3-4 panels, weekday, fast) and the Sunday strip (full-page, 8-12 panels, title panel at top, deeper engagement). Euangelion would adopt both rhythms — matching both the Lord's-Day liturgical instinct and the newspaper-history instinct.

Sunday strip ideas held in reserve: Lazarus / "Come out" (four wordless panels), The Five Thousand ("five loaves. two fish. one kid."), Walking on Water ("as long as he looked — he walked"), The Narrow Gate. Each would be hand-curated, generated in batches per liturgical season.

Sign the lineage, own the originality

None of Peanuts, B.C., Family Circus, or Calvin & Hobbes is in the public domain. Aesthetic inspiration is legal; visual copying is not. The defensible posture: "We owe Schulz, Hart, Keane, and Watterson. We are not them." The riso/halftone duotone brand spec (cobalt + cream + crimson) is already different enough from any single existing strip that the lineage is honored without infringement. The protagonist is a new character with no name yet — the founder names her.

03 · Soul Audit kicker

Should Soul Audit get a LETTERS TO THE EDITOR sub-kicker?

A small italic byline above the SOUL AUDIT kicker. Three-word change. Reinforces the metaphor — or quietly undermines a section just reverted to default styling.

PRO — Ship it.

Doubles down on the metaphor where it matters. Soul Audit is most exposed to "this is software" framing (textarea, prompt pills, submit). LETTERS TO THE EDITOR reframes the same UI as writing in to the paper.

Pairs the section with the bento LETTERS tile. Reader writes a letter; the editor (Soul Audit) reads it.

Three-word change, reversible in 30 seconds. No layout shift, no styling change.

CON — Leave it alone.

The section was just reverted. Adding theming right after stripping creates a yo-yo signal. The section now wants to recede visually.

"SOUL AUDIT" is a strong, plain kicker. Reframing as a letter to the editor reads cute on first pass, then strains every time a returning user uses it as an actual tool.

The metaphor already does enough work elsewhere. Restraint is a feature.

My recommendation

Ship the LETTERS bento tile that funnels into Soul Audit; do not add a sub-kicker on Soul Audit itself.

Book-spine Progress

S

A thin gilt rule in the gutter fills as you read.

Replaces the existing horizontal scroll bar with a vertical hairline, gold. Reads like the spine of a book.

Live · scroll inside this stage

Most days are five-to-seven minutes. The reader picks one passage, sits with it, and walks on. The metaphor of a paper is that today's edition is finite — you do not have to read everything.

The spine on the right fills as you scroll this little column. On the homepage, it would fill across the whole page. Quiet measure of progress, never a streak number.

Continue scrolling… the gold rule keeps ticking up. When you reach the bottom of an article, the rule reaches the bottom of the gutter. That is the only progress metric.

Designed to be quieter than a scroll bar. Cobalt rail with gold fill, hairline width. No labels, no percentages. The reader feels their pace without being measured.

My pick: Ship v1
Decision

What this trade looks like, item by item.

Gained — density

One screen-height now carries 7 tiles instead of 2 cards. Returning readers see today's headline, scripture, funnies, opinion, and letters without scrolling.

Gained — daily ritual

The dateline + ticker + funnies say "today's edition is its own thing." First-time visitors feel they've arrived at today's paper, not at a static brochure.

Gained — funnies slot

The strip is on the front page, not in a sub-route. Matches Le Monde + Plantu (49 years on the front). Reader doesn't have to hunt for it.

Lost — Soul Audit prominence

The current giant Soul Audit kicker shrinks to a small Letters tile. Less aggressive, more publication-feeling. The tool is still one tap away.

Lost — hero-image dominance

The single big hero shrinks to a banded roll-the-stone band. Less "campaign landing page," more "front page."

Lost — visual breathing room

The current page has more white space per visible item. The bento is denser. Density is the trade.

Risk — bento blurs hierarchy

Research warns bento on editorial blurs date/prominence. Mitigation: the lead tile is 2× the rest; the funnies tile is sized intentionally smaller. We use bento as marketing-page grammar, not editorial-reader grammar.

Risk — mobile compression

On a phone, the 7-tile grid collapses to a single column. The mobile previews in section 08 show how each tile reads at 320px-wide.

Risk — Soul Audit conversion

If the LETTERS tile converts worse than the current giant kicker, the gain isn't worth the loss. Mitigation: ship with both options behind a flag; A/B for 2 weeks; pick the winner.

Which side wins?

Skip / Reserve — fights the brand or the budget

Direction D (Two on a Rock) — skip entirely. The B.C. register is too gag-heavy for the devotional voice we're building.

Soundscape toggle — Awwwards staple but ambient audio fights the contemplative reading register. Skip.

Long-scroll dateline tomorrow-fade · Reading-time gated reveals · Kinetic letter scatter — fight the "no engagement bait" founder rule.

Audio Edition · Marginalia · Dear Abby — premature without a base.

The cadence promise — what this commits us to

  • 30 strips per liturgical season, generated in batches, hand-curated.
  • Single writer for caption authorship — placeholder until founder decides whether that voice is the founder or a hire.
  • Weekly editorial review before strips go live.
  • No real-time AI generation on user request. Per CLAUDE.md "manifest first, generation last."
  • The protagonist needs a name. Working placeholder: "the dark-haired kid." Founder picks the name.
